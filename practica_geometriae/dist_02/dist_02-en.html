Second Distinction — On the Finding of Roots¶

Latin Locus: Distinctio II (pp. 18–30) · Commentary: Notes on Square-Root Extraction

Second distinction. Here begins the chapter on the finding of roots.

When we wish to measure fields by geometrical rules, it is necessary for us in certain measurements to find the roots of numbers. Wherefore I have taken care to demonstrate presently how the roots of numbers are found, so far as seems sufficient for this work. For the root of a number is the side of some square; because when a side is multiplied into itself, the area (embadum) of that square is completed: wherefore the sum of the multiplication of the side is rightly called a square, since it contains in itself a square surface.

And it is to be noted that one figure is the root of numbers of one figure and of two; two

f. 0026 · p. 19

figures are the root of three and four; three figures are the root of five figures and six; four figures are the root of seven figures and eight, and so forth. Now the roots of square numbers of one figure and of two must be known by heart, so that we may proceed more subtly with them in finding the roots of many figures. For 1 is the root of 1, because once one makes one; and 2 is the root of 4, and 3 of 9, and 4 of 16, and 5 of 25, and 6 of 36, and 7 of 49, and 8 of 64, and 9 of 81, and 10 of 100. But the remaining numbers, which lie below these square numbers, have no root. Yet we can subtly approximate their root with fractions as closely as is necessary in several ways; which will be demonstrated in its proper place. And when you wish to find the whole root of any number of three figures, find first the root of the last figure; and set it under the second place, since you know two figures are necessary in the root of three figures; and thus one will be under the second place, and the other will fall under the first: and whatever remains over from that third figure, set above that same third figure; and join that excess with the second figure; and set another figure under the first place, namely before the figure that is placed under the second; which, being multiplied by double the figure placed in the root, may come so near to the said joined number that from the remainder joined with the first figure you may subtract the multiplication of that figure into itself, and there does not remain from it more than double the root found: and if nothing remains from the last figure, you will understand to happen for the second figure that which we said of joining the excess of the third figure with the second. For example: we wish to find the root of 153; you will find the root of 1, which is in the third place, which is 1; set this 1 under 5, and before this 1 set 2 under 3; because when 2 is multiplied into double the root found, namely into 2, they make 4; which being subtracted from 5, there remains 1 above that 5; and joining this 1 with the 3 of the first place makes 13; from which we can subtract the multiplication of 2 into itself, and there remains 9, which is less than double the root found, namely 24: therefore the whole root of 153 is 12, and 9 remains. Likewise, if you wish to find the root of 864, set 2 under 6, since 2 is the whole root of 8; and set the 4 that remains above 8: then double this 2, making 4, which set under 2; and by this 4 divide 46, namely the joining of the excess of the third figure with the second, and 11 will come out: from which division we can form a judgment of the next figure to be placed, which is to be multiplied by double the first placed figure, and afterwards by itself; that figure will be either a little less, or as much as comes from that division, which you will know from practice. Wherefore by judgment we shall set 9 under the first figure, which is less than the aforesaid 11; and you will multiply 9 by 4, namely by double the two found, and subtract from the aforesaid 46, and from the remaining 10 set 0 above 6, and 1 above 4; and join that 10 with the 4 of the first place, making 104; from which, having subtracted the multiplication of nine into itself, there remain 23; which 23 is less than double the root found.

For if you desire to know how many figures will be in the root of some number of many figures, consider whether the number of figures of that number is even or odd. If it be even, halve that number; and as many units as are in the half, so many figures will be in its root. But if it be odd, add one to the number of them, so that the number of figures may be made even. And then as many units as are in the half of the number of them, so many figures will likewise be in the root. And then you will begin to set the first figure under that place under which it falls. Now if you desire to know near the truth the fractions that fall from the excess of the whole roots found, consider whether that number for which you have found the root be of perticae according to geometry, or of degrees according to astronomy.

Because in roots of perticae it is necessary to reduce the fractions into pedes, then into unciae, and into parts of unciae. In roots of degrees also the fractions are to be reduced into minutes, and into seconds, and into parts of seconds. For a pertica, as we said, is 6 pedes; and a pes is 18 unciae; and an uncia is \(\frac{1}{18}\) of a pes; and a pes is a sixth of a pertica; the whole pertica is 108 unciae. Likewise, a degree is divided into 60 minutes, and a minute into 60 seconds, and a second similarly into 60 thirds; wherefore a whole degree is 3600 seconds, and 216000 thirds: having recalled these things to memory, when you wish to find the root of any number of perticae, multiply those perticae by the multiplication of 108 times 108, that is, by 11664; and find the root of the sum of the multiplication; and you will have the number of unciae that are in the root sought: which unciae, being divided by 18, give the pedes that are in that root. Which pedes, divided by 6, give perticae. For example: we wish to find the root of 67 perticae; multiply 67 by 11664, making 781488; of which find the root, which is 884, and 32 remains: divide this 32 by double 884, or divide half of 32, namely 16, by 884, and nearly \(\frac{1}{55}\) will come out: therefore the root of 67 perticae is 884 unciae and \(\frac{1}{55}\); which unciae, divided by 18, give 49 pedes, and 2 unciae and \(\frac{1}{55}\) remain: which 49 pedes, divided by 6, give 8 perticae, and 1 pes remains: therefore the root of 67 perticae is 8 perticae, and 1 pes, and 2 unciae, and \(\frac{1}{55}\); and so take care to do in all similar cases. And note those things which we said above concerning the multiplication of perticae, and pedes, and unciae into perticae, and pedes, and unciae; and you will know whence proceed those things which we do in finding pedes and unciae in roots.

Likewise, if you wish to find the root of 1234 perticae. Its whole root is 35 perticae, and 9 perticae remain; which 9 perticae are 54 pedes: through which 54 pedes, since it is less than double the root found, namely 70, we know that in this root no pedes at all fall: wherefore from 54 pedes you will make unciae, making 972 unciae; which divide by 70, and 13 unciae and \(\frac{7}{8}\) will come out; and nothing remains from it whence you may subtract the multiplication of \(13\ \frac{7}{8}\) unciae into themselves: wherefore for \(\frac{7}{8}\) set less, namely \(\frac{6}{7}\), or \(\frac{5}{6}\), or \(\frac{4}{5}\), or \(\frac{3}{4}\) at will; because doing thus, you will be able to deviate very little; and thus, according to this manner, you can subtly find the root for any number of perticae, or of perticae and pedes, and also of perticae, and pedes, and unciae.

Indeed, concerning the finding of fractions of degrees in minutes and seconds, in our book of the abacus we have shown clearly enough; truly indeed the roots of numbers which are not square can be had in lines, but in numbers not at all. For the manner of finding roots in lines is that you draw a line of that quantity, or number, whose root

you desire to have, and add upon it a line of one unit, and fix the center in the middle of that line, and with the interval of half the line draw a circle; and from the point which is between the unit and the line, draw a line at right angles as far as the circle; and that line will be the true root of the number sought. For example: we wish to find the root of 67. Let a certain straight line .ab. lie adjacent, which is 67; to which let the unit .ag. be added in a straight line, and the whole .gb. will be 68: and let .gb. be divided into two equal parts at the point .d.; and with interval .dg. or .db. let the circle .gebz. be described. And let the line .ae. be drawn at right angles; I say that line .ae. is the true root of line .ab., namely of 67.

Let the straight line .ea. be drawn in a straight line to the point .z.; and since straight line .ba. stands upon straight line .ez., it makes two angles either right, or equal to two right angles. Right indeed is that which is under .bae., right also is that which is under .baz. And since the straight line .ag. drawn through the center cuts a certain straight line .ez. at right angles, it is necessary that it cut that straight line into equal parts. Therefore straight line .az. is equal to straight line .ea. Again, since in the circle .gebz. two straight lines, which are .bg. and .ez., cut one another upon the point .a., the multiplication of .ba. into .ag. will be as the multiplication of .ea. into .az. But the multiplication of .ae. into .az. is as the multiplication of .ea. into itself. Wherefore the multiplication of .ba. into .ag. is as the multiplication of .ae. into itself. Indeed the multiplication of .ba. into .ag., namely of 67 into 1, makes 67. And truly the multiplication of .ae. into itself likewise makes 67; and this we wished.

On the Multiplication of Roots¶

Now let us show how roots of numbers are multiplied by roots. And how they are added together. And in what way the less are subtracted from the greater. Also and in what order they are divided among themselves. When you wish to multiply the root of 16 by the root of 25, since 16 and 25 are square numbers: take their roots, which are 4 and 5; and multiply them together, and 20 will come out for the sought multiplication. But if you wish to multiply the root of 16 by the root of 30; since the root of 30 is surd, you will multiply 16 by 30, making 480; of which find the root as closely as you can, and you will have the sum of the said multiplication. Again, if you wish to multiply the root of 20 by the root of 30, multiply 20 by 30, making 600; of which find the root, and you will have what was proposed. For example: let .a. be 20, and .b. 30, and let .g. be the root of 20, and .d. be the root of 30; let .a. multiplied into .b. make .e., namely 600; and let .g. multiplied into .d. make .z. I say that .z. is the root of six hundred, namely of .e. But first it must be said that as any number is to any number, so is any multiple of the one to the same multiple of the other; and whatever part of the one to the same part of the other: which we shall prove in 12 and 24: for in what ratio 12 is to 24, in the same is the double of 12 to the double of 24, and the triple to the triple, and thereafter the quadruple, and the multiples that follow. Similarly, in what ratio 12 is to 24, in the same ratio is half of 12 to half of 24, and a third to a third, and so on: these things therefore being demonstrated, let us return to what was proposed. Since the number .g. is the root of the number .a., namely of 20; therefore .g., multiplying itself, makes .a.; and .g. indeed multiplying .d. makes the number .z. Therefore as great a multiple as .a. is of .g., so great a multiple is .z. of .d.: wherefore as .g. is to .d., so .a. is to .z. Again, since .d. is the root of .b., namely of 30; therefore when .d. multiplies itself, it makes .b. But .d. multiplying .g. makes .z. Therefore as great a multiple as .z. is of .g., so great a multiple is .b. of .d. Wherefore as .g. is to .d., so .z.

is to .b.: therefore it was as .g. to .d., so .a. to .z.: by equality then it is as .a. to .z., so .z. to .b. Wherefore the multiplication of .a. into .b. is as the multiplication of .z. into itself. But the multiplication of .a. into .b. makes .e., namely 600. And the multiplication of .z. into itself likewise makes .e. Therefore .z. is the root of .e.; which it was necessary to show. And it is to be noted that when numbers whose roots are multiplied together have among themselves the ratio of square numbers, their multiplication rises to a square number. Wherefore the multiplication of their roots rises to a rational number. For example: we wish to multiply the root of 8 by the root of 18; whose ratio is as 4 to 9, namely as a square number to a square number. I say that the multiplication of 8 into 18 rises to a square number, namely to 144; whose root, which is 12, is the sum of the multiplication of the root of 8 into the root of 18. Likewise, if you wish to multiply 10 by the root of 20, multiply the square of 10, namely 100, by 20, making 2000; whose root is the sum of the said multiplication. And note that what comes forth from the multiplication of 10 into the root of 20 is equal to 10 roots of 20. Whence when we wish to reduce 10 roots of 20 into the root of one number, then we multiply the square of 10 into 20, and we have 2000; whose root is that which is gathered from those 10 roots: and the same is to be understood of similar cases: whence if you wish to reduce the root of some number into several roots of another, divide that number by some square number; and as many units as are in the root of that square number, so many roots will come of the number coming out from the division. For example: you wish to reduce the root of 1200 into several roots of another number; you will divide 1200 by some square number, let us say by 16, and 75 will come; and take the root of 16, which is 4; and so many roots of 75 you will have for one root of 1200: and if we divide 1200 by 25, whose root is 5, we shall have 5 roots of 48 for one root of 1200. And thus we can reduce the root of 1200 into several roots of different numbers.

On the Addition of Roots¶

Roots of numbers are sometimes so joined with roots that the sum of their joining rises to some rational number, or to the root of some number; and sometimes they cannot be joined so that a number or the root of a number comes out from their joining.

When we wish to join the roots of squares into one, then from their joining a number is produced: as if we wish to add the root of 16 with the root of 25, we shall unite 4 and 5, namely the roots of 16 and of 25, and 9 will come out for the sought joining.

But when we wish to join the roots of numbers having among themselves the ratio of squares, then the root of some rational number comes out. Whence let us show in two ways how they are added. We wish to add the root of 27 with the root of 48; find the squares which are in their ratio, and they will be 9 and 16; whose roots add into one, making 7; which multiply into itself, making 49; which triple on account of 27 and 48, which are triple 9 and 16, making 147; whose root is the sum of the aforesaid joining. Which rule is proved by proportions of similar triangles in this manner: let straight lines .ab. and .bg. lie adjacent in a straight line; of which let .ab. be the root of 27, and .bg. of 48; and from point .a., making an angle, let straight line .ad.de. be drawn; and let .ad. be 3, and let .de. be 4; and let .eg.

f. 0034 · p. 27

and .db. be joined: therefore the square of line .ab. is triple the square of line .ad., and the square of line .bg. is triple the square of line .de. Therefore as straight line .ab. is to .ad., so straight line .bg. is to .de. Wherefore straight line .db. is parallel to line .eg. Whence triangle .adb. is similar to triangle .aeg.; they have angle .a. common; and angle .adb. is equal to angle .aeg., and angle .adb. to angle .age., namely exterior to interior. Therefore as the square of line .ab. is to the square of line .ad., so the square of line .ag. is to the square of line .ae. But the square of line .ab. is triple the square of line .ad.; wherefore the square of line .ag. is likewise triple the square of line .ae.: but the square of line .ae. is 49; wherefore the square of line .ag. is triple, namely 147. Therefore from the joining of lines .ab. and .bg. comes out the root of 147, as it was necessary to show. And note that all roots of numbers having ratios among themselves can be reduced to roots of one number, by dividing the antecedent number by the antecedent square, and the consequent by the consequent. For example: if you wish to reduce the roots of 27 and of 48 into roots of one number, you will divide the antecedent number, namely 27, by the antecedent square, namely by 9; and the following 48 by the following square, namely by 16, and 3 will come out from each division. Therefore as many units as are in the root of 9, so many roots of 3 are in the root of 27; and as many units as are in the root of 16, so many roots of 3 are in the root of 48; and thus for one root of 27 there are had three roots of 3; and for one root of 48 there are had 4 roots of 3. Whence if we wish to gather them together, we shall have 7 roots of 3 for their aggregation: which if you wish to reduce to the root of one number, multiply the square of 7 by 3; and take the root of that which comes forth; and for their aggregation you will have the root of 147, as above. Otherwise let 27 first be joined with 48, making 75; and let 27 be multiplied by 48, and double the root of that multiplication, making 72; which add with 75, and you will likewise have 147 for the square of the said joining.

Which also we shall demonstrate by an example: let straight line .ab. lie adjacent in a straight line to .bg.; and let .ab. be the root of 27, and .bg. of 48. Therefore we wish to know the quantity of the whole line .ag. Since line .ag. is divided into two at point .b., the two squares of the portions .ab. and .bg. with double the multiplication of .ab. into .bg. will be equal to the square of the whole line .ag. But the squares of .ab. and .bg. are 27 and 48, namely 75; and the multiplication of line .ab. into .bg. rises to the root of the multiplication of 27 into 48, namely to 36; whose double is 72; which being added with 75, give 147 for the square of line .ag., as we said before. But if we wish to add the root of 20 with the root of 30; because 20 and 30 do not have among themselves the ratio as a square number to a square number; and this is known because the multiplication of 20 into 30 rises to a number not having a root. Therefore the sum of the joining of those roots does not rise to a number, nor to the root of a number. For example: let straight line .ba. be the root of 20, and let straight line .ag. be the root of 30; the square of line .ba. with the square of line .ag. will be 50; and the multiplication of .ba. into .ag. rises to the root of 600; wherefore double the multiplication of .ba. into .ag. rises to the root of four times 600, namely to the root of 2400: which since they have no root, we know that from the aforesaid joining we cannot have a number, or the root of a number; but we have for their joining the root of a number and of a root, that is, the root of 50, and of the root of 2400. Whence, that we may have from it that which we can, let the root of 2400 be found as closely as possible, which is 49 minus \(\frac{1}{98}\); and let 50 be added to the aforesaid, making 99

f. 0035 · p. 28

minus \(\frac{1}{98}\); of which you will find the root, and you will have what was proposed: or you will find the root of 20 and of 30 as closely as you can; and add them together, and you will likewise have what was proposed.

On the Subtraction of Roots¶

If you wish to subtract the root of a square number from the root of a square number, as the root of 16 from the root of 49, you will subtract the root of 16, namely 4, from the root of 49, namely from 7, and 3 will remain for the residue of the said subtraction. And if you wish to subtract the root of 45 from the root of 125; because their ratio is the ratio of squares, namely of 9 and of 25, subtract the root of 9 from the root of 25, namely 3 from 5, and 2 remains; which multiply into itself, making 4; which multiply by 5 on account of 45 and 125, which are fivefold of 9 and of 25, and 20 will come out; whose root is the residue of the said subtraction. For example: let straight line .ab. lie adjacent, which is the root of 125; and from point .a. let straight line .ag. be drawn making angle .bag.; and let .ag. be 5, namely the root of 25; and let straight line .gd. be taken away from .ag., which is 3, and .da. will remain 2; and through point .d. let straight line .de. be drawn parallel to side .gb.; and since in triangle .agb. straight line .de. has been drawn parallel to side .bg., the sides .ab. and .ag. will be proportionally cut at points .de., namely that it is as .ae. to .eb., so .ad. to .dg. Composedly therefore it is as .ba. to .be., so .ga. to .gd.: and if we permute, it will be as .ba. to .ag., so .be. to .gd. Wherefore it is as the square of side .ba. to the square of side .ag., so the square of line .be. to the square of line .gd. But the square of side .ba. is fivefold the square of side .ag.; wherefore the square of straight line .be. is also fivefold the square of line .gd.: for the square of line .gd. is 9; wherefore the square of line .be. is 45: therefore line .be. is the root of 45; which we wish to subtract from .ab., namely from the root of 125, and to find the residue, namely line .ae.; whose square has ratio to the square of line .ad., as the square of line .be. to the square of line .gd. Wherefore the square of line .ae. is fivefold the square of line .ad. But the square of line .ad. is 4; therefore the square of line .ae., namely of the sought residue, is 20; and thus .ae. has been found to be the root of 20, as above. Or if we reduce the roots of 45 and of 125 into roots of 5, we shall have for the root of 45 three roots of 5, and for the root of 125 we shall have 5 roots of 5: whence if from 5 roots of 5 there be taken away 3 roots of 5, 2 roots of 5 remain, which are one root of 20, as has been found. Otherwise add 45 with 125, making 170; from which you will subtract double the root of the multiplication of 45 into 125, that is 150, and 20 remains for the square of the sought residue. For an example of which let line .ab. be the root of 125; from which let line .bg. be taken, which is the root of 45: I say that line .ga. is the root of 20. Since line .ab. is divided into two at point .g., the two squares of lines .ab. and .gb. will be equal to the square of .ag., and double the multiplication of .gb. into .ab.: for the two squares of .ab. and .gb. are 170; to which the square of line .ag. and double the multiplication of .bg. into .ba. are equal. But .bg. into .ba. is the root of the multiplication of 45 into 125, namely 75; which 75 doubled makes 150; which subtracted from 170 leaves 20 for the square of line .ag.; which it was necessary to show. And if you wish to subtract the root of 20 from the root of 30; since 20 and 30 are not in the ratio of square numbers: you will find the root of 20 and of 30 as closely as you can; and subtract the root of 20 from the root of 30; and thus you will have the residue not fully, but nearly: or otherwise add 20 with 30, making 50; and multiply 20 by 30, making 600; from which take two roots, namely double its root, that is, the root of four times

f. 0036 · p. 29

600, which is nearly 49 minus \(\frac{1}{98}\); and subtract it from 50, and \(1\ \frac{1}{98}\) remains; of which find the root, and you will have the sought residue, according to approximation.

On the Division of Roots¶

If you wish to divide the root of 600 by the root of 40, divide 600 by 40, and 15 will come out; whose root falls from the aforesaid division. For example: let .a. be 40, and .b. 600, and .g. 15; and let .d. be the root of .a., and .e. of .b.: let .e. be divided by .d., namely the root of 600 by the root of 40, and let .z. come out. I say that .z. is the root of .g., namely of 15; since when .e. is divided by .d. it makes .z., if .d. multiplies .z., it makes .e. But .d. multiplying itself makes .a. As great a multiple therefore as .a. is of .d., so great a multiple is .e. of .z. Wherefore it is as .d. to .z., so .a. to .e. Again, .z. multiplying .d. makes .e.; and .z. multiplying itself makes .I. Therefore it is as .d. to .z., so .e. to .I. But as .d. to .z., so .a. to .e. has been found. Wherefore it is as .a. to .e., so .e. to .I. Therefore .a., .e., .i. are continuously proportional. Wherefore the multiplication of .a. into .I. is as .e. into itself. But the multiplication of .e. into itself makes .b.; therefore .a. into .I. likewise makes .b., namely 600. But when .b. is divided by .a., .g. comes. Therefore the multiplication of .a. by .g. makes .b.; and .a. multiplied by .I. makes .b. Wherefore the number .I. is equal to the number .g. But .z. multiplying itself makes .I. Wherefore from the multiplication of .z. into itself will likewise come out .g.; therefore .z. is the root of .g., which it was necessary to show. Otherwise: .d. multiplying .z. makes .e.; and .e. multiplying itself makes .b.; therefore the multiplication of .d. into .z. multiplied by .e. makes .b. Likewise .d. multiplying itself makes .a.; and .a. multiplying .g. makes .b.; therefore the multiplication of .d. into itself, multiplied by .g., makes .b. Wherefore the multiplication of .d. into .z. brought into .e. is equal to the multiplication of .d. into itself brought into .g.: let the multiplication of .d. be left aside in common, and the multiplication of .z. into .e. will remain equal to the multiplication of .d. into .g. Wherefore it is as .d. to .z., so .e. to .g., but as .d. to .z., so .e. is to .I.; therefore .e. has the same ratio to .g. and to .I.: wherefore .g. is equal to .I. But .z. is the root of .I.; wherefore .z. will be the root of .g. And if you wish to divide the root of 40 by the root of 600, divide 40 by 600, and \(\frac{1}{15}\) will come out; of which find the root, and you will have what was proposed. Now we shall take care to demonstrate how roots are found for fractions. But first it is to be noted that when the roots of square numbers, or of those having their ratio, are divided among themselves, then a rational number always comes out. For example: we wish to divide the root of 64 by the root of 16: when 64 is divided by 16, 4 comes out; whose root, namely 2, comes out from the said division. For 8, which is the root of 64, divided by the root of 16, namely by 4, likewise comes out 2. And note that the same comes out from the division of the roots of all numbers having the same ratio that 16 has to 64: as if we wish to divide the root of 80 by the root of 20, 2 will likewise come out from the said division. If you desire to find a root for some fraction or fractions, we shall demonstrate to you in two ways how to do this. The first way is that you take that part, or parts, from some large number; and whatever you shall have, multiply by that number; and find the root of the sum of the multiplication; and divide it by the aforesaid number; and thus you will have what was proposed. For example: we wish to find the root of \(\frac{2}{3}\): take \(\frac{2}{3}\) from some large number. And let that number be 60. For the larger the number you take, the nearer you will come to the true root: indeed \(\frac{2}{3}\) of 60 is 40; which multiplied by 60 gives 2400; whose root, which is 49, minus

f. 0037 · p. 30

\(\frac{1}{98}\), divide by 60, and you will have what was proposed. For if you desire to have this in pedes and unciae, take \(\frac{2}{3}\) from the unciae of one pertica, namely from 108, and 72 will come out; which multiplied by 108 come to 7776; of which take the root, and it will be \(88\ \frac{2}{11}\); and so many unciae are in the root of \(\frac{2}{3}\) of one pertica. Similarly, if you wish to have in minutes and seconds the root of \(\frac{4}{5}\) of one degree, take \(\frac{4}{5}\) from the seconds of one degree, namely from 3600, making 2880; which multiply by 3600, making 10368000 fourths; whose root will give you the seconds that are in the root of \(\frac{4}{5}\).

Otherwise we wish to find the root of \(\frac{2}{3}\) of one pertica; because from the multiplication of a pes into a pes a denarius comes out. Therefore make denarii of \(\frac{2}{3}\) of one pertica, making 24 denarii; of which find the root, and 4 pedes will come out, and 8 remains; from which make eighteenths of one denarius, making 144; which divide by double the root found, namely by 8, and 16 unciae will come out, and \(\frac{16}{18}\) remains: which multiply by 18, making \(\frac{288}{324}\); from which subtract the multiplication of 16 unciae into themselves, namely \(\frac{256}{324}\), and \(\frac{8}{81}\) of one denarius will remain; which divided by double the root found, there will come out nearly \(\frac{2}{11}\) of one uncia. Or otherwise: take the root of 24 denarii, and it will be 5 pedes, and 1 denarius is lacking to you; from which make eighteenths, making 18; which divide by double the root found, namely by 10, and \(1\ \frac{4}{5}\) uncia will come out; which subtracted from 5 pedes, there remain 4 pedes, and \(16\ \frac{1}{5}\) unciae.
