Epistle and Prologue to Master Dominicus
----------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Prologus (p. 1) <dist-01-prol-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on the Prologue <dist-01-s1-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   Here begins the *Practice of Geometry* composed by :person:`Leonardo of Pisa <fibonacci>`, of the sons of Bonaccio, in the year 1220.

You requested, my friend :person:`Dominicus <dominicus>` and reverend Master, that I compose for you a book on the practice of geometry; constrained therefore by your friendship and yielding to your entreaties,
I have now published for your sake a work begun long ago, so that both those who wish to work in measurements according to geometrical demonstrations and those who wish to proceed according to common custom, as it were in a lay manner, may find perfect instruction across the eight distinctions of this art,
which are unfolded below. The first of these is how the breadths of fields having four equal angles are multiplied in three ways into their lengths. The second is on certain geometrical rules and on the finding of square roots, so far as I thought necessary for those who wish to work by geometrical reasons alone.
The third is on finding the areas of all fields of whatever form. The fourth is on the division of all fields among co-heirs (:term:`consortes`). The fifth is on finding cube roots. The sixth is on finding the areas of all solid bodies of whatever figure, which are contained by three dimensions,
namely length, breadth, and depth. The seventh is on finding the lengths of planes and the heights of elevated things. The eighth is on certain geometrical subtleties. Yet before I arrive at the doctrine of these distinctions, I thought it necessary to set down beforehand certain introductory matters.
Therefore, having undertaken to complete these things according to the capacity of my intellect and trusting in your correction, I have taken care to address this work to your mastery, so that whatever in it needs emendation may be corrected by your wisdom.

Introductory Definitions
------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Introductoria (pp. 1–3) <dist-01-intro-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Definitions & Pisan Metrology <dist-01-s2-notes>`

A **point** is that which has no dimension, that is, which cannot be divided. A **line** is length lacking breadth, whose extremities are points.
A **straight line** is that which is drawn straight from point to point. A **surface** is that which has breadth and length only, whose boundaries are lines; and it is plane when it is spread out evenly within its boundaries upon straight lines. A **plane angle** is the inclination of two lines touching one another in a plane,
when they do not lie in a straight line; and it is rectilinear when the lines containing the angle are straight. And when a straight line stands upon a straight line and makes two angles about itself equal to one another, each angle is called **right**; and the line standing upon that on which it stands
is called :term:`cathetus` or perpendicular. An ample or obtuse angle is one that is greater than a right angle. An acute angle is found to be less than a right angle. And a boundary is the end of a thing. A figure is that which lies under one or more boundaries. A rectilinear figure is surrounded by straight lines.
Trilateral figures are those contained under three straight lines. Quadrilateral figures are those encompassed by four straight lines. Multilateral figures are those comprehended under more than four sides. A **circle** is a plane figure contained under one line, which is called the circumference or periphery, within which there is a point from which all straight lines
drawn to the circumference are equal to one another; and that point is called the center of the circle. And when any straight line is drawn through the center and terminated at both sides of the periphery,

that straight line is called the diameter of the circle,
which divides the circle into two equal portions, each of which is called a semicircle. A segment of a circle is a figure contained under the periphery of the circle and a straight line, whether it be greater or less than a semicircle. A sector of a circle is a plane figure contained under two straight lines
drawn from the center to the periphery and the arc of the periphery comprehended by those straight lines. Straight lines which are in the same surface and, produced indefinitely on both sides, never meet one another are called parallel; if any straight line falls upon them, it will make the two interior angles on one side right
or equal to two right angles, and the exterior angle is equal to the interior angle opposite to it, and the alternate angles are equal to one another: as if upon two parallel lines `.a.b.` and `.g.d.` there falls a straight line `.e.z.`; the angles `.b.i.z.` and `.i.z.d.` are either right or equal to two right angles.
The same holds for angles `.a.i.z.` and `.i.z.g.`; and the exterior angle `.e.i.b.` is equal to the interior angle `.i.z.d.` opposite to it; and angle `.a.i.z.` is equal to that which lies alternate to it, namely angle `.i.z.d.`; hence angle `.b.i.z.` is also equal to angle `.i.z.g.`, as is shown in geometry.
Many things indeed must be known by those who wish to proceed according to geometrical subtlety in the measurement and division of bodies, which are clearly demonstrated in :person:`Euclid <euclid>`. Among these are: to construct an equilateral triangle upon a given finite straight line; to bisect a given angle or line;
to erect a perpendicular upon a given straight line from a given point on it; and to draw a perpendicular to a given straight line from a given point not on it. Also, a straight line standing upon a straight line makes two angles about itself equal to two right angles. And when two straight lines intersect each other,
the two angles vertically opposite are equal to one another. And when a straight line falls upon two straight lines and makes on one side two interior angles less than two right angles, if those straight lines are produced on that side where the angles are less than two right angles, they will meet one another.
And if two straight lines join the ends of two equal and parallel straight lines, they themselves will be equal and parallel to one another. And upon a given straight line, at a given point on it, to construct a rectilinear angle equal to a given rectilinear angle. And through a given point to draw a straight line parallel to a given straight line.
And triangles and parallelograms constructed on equal bases or on the same base between the same parallels are equal to one another, triangle to triangle and parallelogram to parallelogram, and the parallelogram is double the triangle. A parallelogram is called a figure which has opposite sides equal and parallel, whose opposite angles are equal to one another,
and a diameter also cuts it into two similar and equal triangles; also, if any side of a triangle be produced on any side, it will make an angle outside the triangle equal to the two opposite interior angles within the triangle; and the three angles of any triangle are equal to two right angles.
And things equal to the same thing are equal to one another. And if equals be added to equals, the wholes are equal. And if equals be taken away from equals, what remain are equal. And if equals be added to unequals, the wholes are unequal. And if equals be taken away from unequals,
the remainders are unequal. And doubles of the same thing are equal to one another, and halves of the same thing are equal to one another; and things fitted upon one another are equal to one another. And the whole is greater than its part. And two straight lines do not enclose a space.
Without all these, and without the finding of roots, those who wish to proceed according to the common manner

The Pisan Pertica and Its Parts
-------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`De Pertica Pisana (pp. 3–5) <dist-01-pertica-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on the 6 × 6 Pertica Lattice <dist-01-s2-notes>`

.. picture:: /_static/figures/pisan_pertica_grid.svg
   :alt: Pisan Superficial Pertica (6 x 6 Denarii Grid)
   :caption: The Pisan superficial pertica < A B D C > of 6 x 6 linear pedes = 36 superficial denarii.

These things having been thus determined, I have proposed to explain briefly with what measures fields are measured. For some are accustomed to measure fields with cubit measures, or ells, or paces; others with rods (:term:`perticae <pertica>`), or with any measurable instrument whatsoever; yet each of the above-written measures is to be understood sometimes as linear,
sometimes as superficial. The linear is that which consists in length alone; the superficial is that which has as much in length as in breadth, existing square in itself and consisting of four right angles. From these superficial measures some in multiplying gather a certain quantity which they call *iugerum*, or *aripennium*, or *carruca*, or *tornatura*,
or *cultura*, or other quantities known by other names. But I, wishing to walk according to the custom of the Pisans, will take my beginning from the :term:`pertica`. The linear Pisan pertica consists of six linear :term:`feet <pes>`, and the linear foot consists of eighteen linear points (:term:`unciae <uncia>`).
Also the square, that is superficial, pertica :g:`ABDC <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>` consists of six superficial feet. For the superficial foot :g:`ABP2P1 <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B P2 P1 >>` (bounded by :g:`P1P2 <pisan_pertica_grid:/ P1 P2 />`) has one pertica in length and one-sixth of a pertica in breadth.
The superficial :term:`uncia` has one pertica in length and one-eighteenth part of the length of a foot in breadth. Wherefore the superficial foot is one-sixth of the superficial pertica, and the superficial uncia is one-eighteenth of the superficial foot, or one-hundred-and-eighth part of the superficial pertica.
Likewise, the superficial pertica contains in itself thirty-six :term:`denarii <denarius>` of measure; and so six denarii belong to each foot, and the superficial uncia is one-third of a denarius. The denarius :g:`CU1U3U2 <pisan_pertica_grid:< C U1 U3 U2 >>` at :g:`C <pisan_pertica_grid:C>` (by :g:`U1 <pisan_pertica_grid:U1>`, :g:`U2 <pisan_pertica_grid:U2>`) has one foot in length and one in breadth,
and thus the square denarius consists of four right angles and is the thirty-sixth part of the whole superficial pertica (whose medians :g:`EF <pisan_pertica_grid:/ E F />` and :g:`GH <pisan_pertica_grid:/ G H />` meet at :g:`I <pisan_pertica_grid:I>`). Four superficial perticae make a measure called a *scala*; five and a half make one :term:`panorum`.
And sixty-six square perticae make a measure called a :term:`stariorum`, by which fields are bought and sold in the bishopric of Pisa, and to which measure I shall show how to gather the :term:`embada <embadum>`, that is, the areas of fields. From the aforesaid stariorum multiplied is gathered another sum called a *modiorum*,
for a *modiorum* contains twenty-four staiora. Also, sixteen and a half scalae make one stariorum, and one scala is one-eleventh of two-thirds of a stariorum, or twelve soldi of measure. Again, twelve panora make one stariorum; so a panorum is one-twelfth of a stariorum, and is sixteen soldi and a half, while a stariorum is one hundred ninety-eight soldi.
Fields and spaces of houses are measured with linear perticae, feet, and unciae; but areas of fields are gathered in stariora, panora, soldi, and denarii, or parts of a panorum, while spaces of houses are gathered in scalae, parts of a scala, soldi, and denarii. Having explained these, it remains to show what results from multiplying perticae and their parts.
Whatever is multiplied into a pertica or perticae yields the same kind: if perticae are multiplied into perticae, perticae result, as three perticae into nine perticae make 27 perticae. And if perticae are multiplied into panora, or panora into perticae, panora result: as three perticae into nine panora, or nine panora into three

.. folio:: boncompagni_1862_vol2_practica_geometriae:0011
   :page: 4

perticae, make 27 panora; and the same is understood of multiplying perticae into scalae, stariora, and modiora, or conversely. Again, feet multiplied into perticae, or perticae into feet, will be feet, that is half-soldi: for example, three feet into nine perticae, or nine perticae into three feet, will be 27 feet, namely 13 and :math:`\frac{1}{2}` soldi.
Likewise, unciae multiplied into perticae, or perticae into unciae, make unciae, or thirds of a denarius: for example, 4 unciae into 9 perticae, or conversely, rise to 36 unciae, that is, 12 denarii of measure. Moreover, feet multiplied into feet rise to denarii: for example, three feet into five feet rise to 15 denarii.
Again, feet multiplied into unciae make eighteenths of one denarius; and unciae multiplied into unciae make eighteenth parts of an eighteenth part of one denarius, namely three-hundred-twenty-fourths of a denarius. And note that the measures multiplied are always linear, and those gathered from their multiplication are superficial.
Furthermore, 100 superficial perticae are one and a half stariora plus one pertica; a hundred scalae are six stariora plus one scala; and a hundred panora are :math:`8\frac{4}{6}` stariora. Also, since :math:`16\frac{1}{2}` soldi are one panorum, 33 soldi are 2 panora, :math:`49\frac{1}{2}` soldi are 3 panora, 66 soldi are 4 panora, :math:`82\frac{1}{2}` soldi are 5 panora,
and 99 soldi are 6 panora. Likewise, :math:`5\frac{1}{2}` scalae are 4 panora, and 11 scalae are 8 panora. Note that I mention scalae because some are accustomed to gather land measures in scalae and make panora from them; hence I leave aside the labor of scalae here and shall teach how to gather land measure
by stariora, panora, soldi, and denarii. Therefore, when :math:`5\frac{1}{2}` perticae are multiplied into any number of perticae, as many panora result as there were perticae into which :math:`5\frac{1}{2}` perticae were multiplied; hence 11 perticae yield twice as many panora, :math:`16\frac{1}{2}` yield thrice, 22 yield fourfold, :math:`27\frac{1}{2}` fivefold, 33 sixfold,
:math:`38\frac{1}{2}` sevenfold, 44 eightfold, :math:`49\frac{1}{2}` ninefold, 55 tenfold, and :math:`60\frac{1}{2}` elevenfold. Likewise, if 66 perticae are multiplied into any number of perticae, twelve times as many panora come out as those perticae, that is, as many stariora as those perticae; wherefore 132 perticae multiplied into any perticae make twice as many stariora,
and so you may understand for similar cases. And this is what I said above about panora, stariora, and modiora multiplied into perticae: for multiplying 22 perticae into 3 perticae is the same as multiplying 4 panora into 3 perticae, and multiplying 132 perticae into any perticae is the same as multiplying 2 stariora into the same.
And note that one pertica in breadth and :math:`5\frac{1}{2}` in length is one panorum; and one pertica in breadth and double :math:`5\frac{1}{2}` in length is double one panorum; and so understand for triple, quadruple, and any multiple of :math:`5\frac{1}{2}` perticae. Likewise, one pertica in breadth and 66 in length is one stariorum.

Wherefore two perticae at the head and 66 perticae in length are 2 stariora; and 3 perticae at the head and 66 in length are 3 stariora; and so in like cases. Again, double one pertica at the head and half of 66 perticae in length is one stariorum;
and triple one pertica at the head and a third of 66 perticae in length is likewise one stariorum; and understand this for any other multiple of one pertica at the head and the corresponding part of 66 perticae in length. Hence if it be asked, when 6 perticae are at the head of some square field,
how many perticae will be in its length to complete a stariorum: since 6 is the sixfold of one pertica, take one-sixth of 66 perticae, namely 11, and you will have the length of that field. Likewise, if there are 7 perticae at the head, a stariorum is completed if in length you have one-seventh of 63 perticae,
giving 9 perticae; and from the three perticae remaining from 63 up to 66 make feet, which will be 18 feet; divide these by 7, and 2 feet come out with 4 feet remaining to be divided; from these make unciae, giving 72 unciae; divide them by 7, and :math:`10\frac{2}{7}` unciae come out.
Thus we have 9 perticae, 2 feet, and :math:`10\frac{2}{7}` unciae for :math:`\frac{1}{7}` of 66 perticae: these things are very useful, as will be demonstrated in their place.

Here Begins the First Distinction, on Multiplying the Breadths of Square Fields into Their Lengths
---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Distinctio I (pp. 5–18) <dist-01-dist1-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Distinctio I <dist-01-s3-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   Here begins the first distinction, on multiplying the breadths of square fields having right angles into their lengths, in which multiplications their areas are contained.

If you wish to measure an equilateral and equiangular quadrilateral field `.a.b.c.d.` having 2 perticae in each side, I say that its area is that which is gathered from the multiplication of side `.a.c.` into side `.a.b.` contiguous to it, namely two perticae into two; and thus its area is 4 superficial perticae.
Let lines `.a.b.` and `.c.d.` be divided into two equal parts at points `.e.`, `.f.`, and draw line `.e.f.`. Likewise let lines `.a.c.` and `.b.d.` be divided into two equal parts at points `.g.`, `.h.`, and draw line `.g.h.`. Thus quadrilateral `.a.b.c.d.` will be divided into four equal right-angled squares,
each of which has one pertica in every side; and thus there are 4 superficial perticae in the whole area of quadrilateral `.a.c.d.b.`. For straight line `.a.e.` is equal and parallel to straight line `.c.f.`, and parallel to line `.a.c.`, and `.e.f.` is equal and parallel to line `.b.d.`, since it is parallel and equal to line `.a.c.`.
Similarly line `.g.h.` will be found parallel and equal to each line `.a.b.` and `.c.d.`. And since line `.e.f.` is parallel to line `.a.c.`, and line `.a.e.` to line `.c.f.`, angle `.a.e.f.` is equal to angle `.a.c.f.` opposite to it; that under `.a.c.f.` is right, hence that under `.a.e.f.` is right;
by the same reasoning angle `.e.f.c.` is shown to be right, since it is equal to the right angle `.c.a.e.`. Wherefore quadrilateral `.e.a.c.f.` is right-angled and contains in itself two square perticae, one of which is quadrilateral `.e.a.g.i.` and the other `.i.g.c.f.`. For by the aforesaid all angles are shown to be right,
including the angles at `.g.` and `.h.`. For surface `.e.c.` contains half of surface `.b.c.`; wherefore surface `.e.c.` is equal to surface `.e.d.`: therefore the area of the whole surface `.a.d.` is 4 perticae, as I said before. Likewise let there be a long rectangular quadrilateral `.a.b.g.d.` having 2 perticae in its opposite heads,
namely `.a.g.` and `.b.d.`, and 3 perticae in each of the remaining sides. I say that its area is 6 perticae, gathered from multiplying one head into one side, namely 2 into 3: to demonstrate this, divide `.a.g.` and `.b.d.` into two equal parts

at points `.e.`, `.z.` and join `.e.z.`;
and divide each of `.a.b.` and `.g.d.` into three equal parts at `.i.`, `.t.` and `.k.`, `.l.`, and join `.i.t.` and `.k.l.`; then by the doctrine of the square above, all `.a.b.g.d.` is shown to be divided into six equal right-angled quadrilaterals, each containing one pertica in every side.
Finally, having shown these things by demonstrations, let us show how the breadths of the said fields ought to be multiplied by their length. If we wish to multiply 7 perticae in breadth by 23 perticae in length, first multiply 7 perticae by 22 perticae, namely by 4 panora, whence 28 panora result by the above reasoning;
then multiply the one pertica remaining from 23 by 7, making 7 square perticae; of which :math:`5\frac{1}{2}` perticae are one panorum, and the :math:`1\frac{1}{2}` pertica remaining is :math:`4\frac{1}{2}` soldi; and thus we have in sum 29 panora and :math:`4\frac{1}{2}` soldi of measure, that is, 2 stariora, 5 panora, and :math:`4\frac{1}{2}` soldi.
Likewise, if you wish to multiply 13 perticae by 31 perticae, first multiply 11 perticae, namely 2 panora, by 31 perticae, making 62 panora, namely double 31; then multiply the 2 perticae remaining from 13 perticae by 31 perticae, making 62 square perticae, which are 11 panora and 18 denarii;
add these to the 62 panora found, making 73 panora and 18 denarii; divide by 12, since a stariorum is 12 panora, and 6 stariora, 1 panorum, and 18 denarii of measure come out. Likewise, if you wish to multiply 19 perticae by 41 perticae, first multiply 19 perticae by 33 perticae,
giving 19 half-stariora, namely :math:`9\frac{1}{2}` stariora; then multiply the 8 remaining from 41 by :math:`16\frac{1}{2}` perticae, giving 24 panora; added to :math:`9\frac{1}{2}` stariora, these make :math:`11\frac{1}{2}` stariora. Also, since :math:`2\frac{1}{2}` perticae remain after taking :math:`16\frac{1}{2}` perticae from 19 perticae,
multiply those :math:`2\frac{1}{2}` perticae again by the aforesaid 8 perticae, making 20 superficial perticae, of which :math:`16\frac{1}{2}` are 3 panora and the remaining :math:`3\frac{1}{2}` are :math:`10\frac{1}{2}` soldi; adding 3 panora and :math:`10\frac{1}{2}` soldi to :math:`11\frac{1}{2}` stariora makes 11 stariora, 9 panora, and :math:`10\frac{1}{2}` soldi.
The cause of this multiplication I wish to declare by demonstrations. Let there be a long quadrilateral `.a.b.g.d.` containing 19 perticae in each head, `.a.b.` and `.g.d.`, and 41 perticae in each of the longer sides, `.a.g.` and `.b.d.`; from side `.a.g.` let line `.a.e.` be taken consisting of 33 perticae, so `.e.g.` will remain 8 perticae.
Likewise from line `.b.d.` let line `.b.z.` be taken equal to line `.a.e.`, and join `.e.z.`; then `.e.z.` will be 19 perticae, since it is equal and parallel to both `.a.b.` and `.g.d.`, and `.z.d.` is equal to line `.e.g.`, namely 8 perticae. Again from lines `.e.z.` and `.g.d.` let straight lines `.e.i.` and `.g.t.` be taken,
each of :math:`16\frac{1}{2}` perticae, and join `.i.t.`; then line `.i.t.` will be equal to both `.e.g.` and `.z.d.`. And when lines `.e.i.` and `.g.t.`, each of :math:`16\frac{1}{2}` perticae, are taken away from `.e.z.` and `.g.d.`, each of `.i.z.` and `.t.d.` will remain :math:`2\frac{1}{2}` perticae.
Therefore when we multiplied 19 perticae above into 33, we had the area of quadrilateral `.a.z.`, namely :math:`9\frac{1}{2}` stariora, and quadrilateral `.e.z.d.g.` remained to us from all `.a.b.g.d.`; then when we multiplied 8 into :math:`16\frac{1}{2}` perticae, namely into 3 panora, we had 2 stariora for the area of quadrilateral `.e.i.t.g.`.
Again, when we multiplied :math:`2\frac{1}{2}` perticae into 8 perticae, we had 20 square perticae, namely 3 panora and :math:`10\frac{1}{2}` soldi of measure, for the area of `.i.z.d.t.`. For the three quadrilaterals `.a.z.`, `.e.t.`, and `.t.z.` equal the whole `.a.b.g.d.`; and this we wished to demonstrate.
Likewise, if

   :page: 7

you wish to multiply 25 perticae by 52 perticae, first multiply 22 perticae of those 25 perticae, namely 4 panora, by 52 perticae, giving 52 thirds of one stariorum because of the 4 panora, which are one-third of one stariorum.

we can otherwise make sixths of one foot from 10 unciae: since 10 unciae are :math:`\frac{10}{18}` of one foot, 10 unciae are :math:`\frac{20}{36}` of a foot; wherefore if we divide 20 by the rule of 36, :math:`\frac{2\ 3}{6\ 6}` of one foot will come out, as we said:
likewise you shall double :math:`14\frac{1}{3}` unciae, giving :math:`\frac{29}{36}` of one foot; and thus you have in the lower side :math:`43\frac{0}{6}\frac{4}{6}\frac{5}{6}` perticae, as we show here: and put six 6s under one bar after :math:`\frac{1\ 0}{11\ 6}` thus, :math:`\frac{0}{6\ 6\ 6\ 6\ 6\ 6\ 11\ 6}`;
and you shall multiply the numbers above the bars and the integers before the bars proceeding forward after the manner of four figures: for example, multiply 2 by 5, which are above the first 6, and divide by the first 6; then 2 by 4 and 5 by 3, and divide by the second 6;
after this 2 by 0, 5 by 1, and 3 by 4, and divide by the third 6; and 2 by 43, 5 by 16, 3 by 0, and 4 by 1, and divide by the fourth 6, and you will have above them the said four sixth parts only of one denarius: then multiply 3 by 43, 4 by 16, and 1 by 0,
and divide by the fifth 6, and you will have denarii above it: then multiply 1 by 43 and 0 by 16, and divide by the sixth 6, and you will have half-soldi above that 6: lastly multiply 16 perticae by 43 perticae, and divide by :math:`\frac{1\ 0}{11\ 6}`, and you will have triple soldi above 11,
double panora above 6, and stariora before the bar; and thus with sixths of sixths of a foot we can proceed indefinitely, reducing the fractions of unciae placed in multiplications into sixths of sixths, if those fractions can fall into them; and if the number of sixths on one side be less than on the other,
you shall supply them with zeros above those sixths, that is, if two sixths are on one side let two be on the other, and if three, three, and so on. And if you cannot at all reduce the fractions of unciae into sixths of sixths, you will by no means be able to work with those fractions by this way;
but leave those fractions aside, and multiply the remainder of those fractions which cannot fall into sixths of sixths, and work from them as we said above.

Since it seems superfluous to demonstrate with our own demonstrations all that :person:`Euclid <euclid>` assigned
concerning proofs, therefore I take care to propose without demonstration certain things from his book necessary for this work. If three numbers or three quantities be proportional, so that as the first number is to the second, so the second is to the third, then the multiplication of the first number into the third
equals the square of the second number, namely its multiplication into itself. For example: 4, 6, and 9 are in one proportion, because as 4 is to 6, so 6 is to 9, or as 9 is to 6, so 6 is to 4. Wherefore the multiplication of 4 into 9 rises to the multiplication of 6 into 6.
Hence when of three given numbers the multiplication of the first into the third makes the square of the second number, then those three numbers are continuously proportional. Also, when four numbers are proportional, and as the first is to the second, so the third is to the fourth, then the multiplication of the first into the fourth
equals the multiplication of the second into the third. For example: as the first number 6 is to the second 8, so the third 9 is to the fourth 12; indeed the multiplication of 6 into 12 will be equal to the multiplication of 8 into 9. For each multiplication rises to 72: from this it is understood
that when the multiplication of the first number into the fourth is equal to the multiplication of the second into the third, then those four numbers are proportional, namely in that very proportion in which

And it must be noted that when any number is divided by any number, if that which comes from the division be multiplied by the divisor, surely the divided number will return from that multiplication: as if 20 be divided by 4, 5 comes out; and from multiplying 4 into 5 the divided number 20 returns.
But if of three proportional numbers two be known, namely the first and the second, and the third be unknown, divide the square of the second number by the first number, and what comes from the division will be the third number. For example: let the first number be 4, the second 6, and we do not know the third.
We shall divide the square of 6, namely 36, by 4, namely by the first number, and 9 will come out for the third number. Wherefore if we multiply the resulting 9 by the divisor 4, 36 will return, namely the divided number: therefore when from multiplying the first number into the third comes out the square of the second number,
then those three numbers are proportional. Therefore as 4 is to 6, so 6 is to 9. For if we do not know the first number, we shall divide the same 36 by the third, 9, and the first, 4, comes out; and if we do not know the second number, we shall multiply the first by the third, namely 4 by 9,
giving 36, whose root, which is 6, is the second number. Likewise let the first number `.a.` be to the second `.b.` as the third `.g.` is to the fourth `.d.`. Since the multiplication of the first into the fourth, namely `.a.` into `.d.`, is equal to the multiplication of `.b.` into `.g.`, namely of the second number into the third:
if `.a.`, `.b.`, and `.g.` be known, and `.d.` be unknown, we shall divide the multiplication of `.b.` into `.g.` by `.a.`, and `.d.` will come out. And if the same multiplication, namely of `.b.` into `.g.`, be divided by `.d.`, `.a.` will come out: for the same reason therefore if the multiplication of `.a.` into `.d.`
be divided by `.b.`, `.g.` will come out; and if it be divided by `.g.`, `.b.` will come out. For example: let `.a.` be 4, `.b.` 6, `.g.` 8, and `.d.` 12; if the multiplication of `.b.` into `.g.`, namely 48, be divided by `.a.`, namely by 4, `.d.` will come out, namely 12;
which 48 if it be divided by 12, namely by the fourth number, 4 will come out, namely the first number. In like manner also, if we divide the multiplication of the first number into the fourth, namely of 4 into 12, that is 48, by the second number, namely by 6, 8 will come out for the third number;
which 48 also divided by the third number, namely by 8, yields the second number. These things therefore having been committed to memory, it must be said: when in a circle two straight lines cut each other, the multiplication of one portion of one line into its other part will equal the multiplication of one portion
of the other line into its remainder. As if in circle `.a.b.g.d.` two straight lines `.a.g.` and `.b.d.` intersect each other at point `.e.`, indeed the multiplication of `.a.e.` into `.e.g.` equals the multiplication of `.b.e.` into `.e.d.`, as is shown in :person:`Euclid <euclid>`. Now let us come to finding the roots of numbers.
