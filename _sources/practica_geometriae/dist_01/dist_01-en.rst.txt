Epistle & Prologue to Master Dominicus
--------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Prologus (p. 1) <dist-01-prol-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on the Prologue <dist-01-s1-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   Here begins the *Practice of Geometry* composed by :person:`Leonardo of Pisa <fibonacci>`, of the sons of Bonaccio, in the year $1220$.

You requested, my friend :person:`Dominicus <dominicus>` and reverend Master, that I compose for you a book on the practice of geometry; constrained therefore by your friendship and yielding to your entreaties, the work begun long ago I have so published for your sake that those who wish to operate in dimensions according to geometrical demonstrations, and those who wish to operate according to common custom after the manner of laymen, may find perfect instruction across the eight distinctions of this art which are set forth below:

1. **First Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-1`): How the breadths of fields having four equal angles are multiplied in three ways into their lengths, and the foundational rules of Pisan land measure.
2. **Second Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-2`): On certain geometric rules and on the finding of square roots, so far as I thought necessary for those wishing to operate by geometrical reasonings alone.
3. **Third Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-3`): On finding the areas (:term:`embada <embadum>`) of all fields of whatever shape—triangles, quadrilaterals, polygons, and circles.
4. **Fourth Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-4`): On the division of all fields among co-heirs or partners (:term:`consortes <consortes>`).
5. **Fifth Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-5`): On finding cube roots.
6. **Sixth Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-6`): On finding the areas and volumes of all solid bodies contained by three dimensions—length, breadth, and depth.
7. **Seventh Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-7`): On finding the lengths of planes and the heights of elevated things through the quadrant (:term:`quadrans`).
8. **Eighth Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-8`): On certain geometrical subtleties pertaining to the regular pentagon, decagon, and irrational lines.

Yet before I arrive at the doctrine of these distinctions, I have thought that certain necessary introductory matters ought to be set down beforehand. In fulfilling these things according to the capacity of my intellect, trusting in your correction, I have taken care to direct this work to your mastery, so that whatever in it needs emendation may be corrected by your wisdom.

Introductory Definitions & Common Conceptions
---------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Introductoria (pp. 1–3) <dist-01-intro-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Definitions & Pisan Metrology <dist-01-s2-notes>`

Before entering upon the distinctions, certain principles, petitions, and common conceptions from the first book of :person:`Euclid <euclid>` (:cite:`euclid:elements`) are pre-established:

* **Point** (*Punctus*): A point is that which has no dimension, that is, which cannot be divided. :just:`[I. Def. 1] <I.def.1>`
* **Line** (*Linea*): A line is length lacking breadth, whose extremities are points. A straight line is that which is drawn straight from point to point, the shortest extension between its points. :just:`[I. Def. 2–4] <I.def.2>`
* **Surface** (*Superficies*): A surface is that which has breadth and length only, whose boundaries are lines; and it is a plane surface when it is spread out evenly within its boundaries upon straight lines. :just:`[I. Def. 5–7] <I.def.5>`
* **Plane Angle** (*Planus angulus*): A plane angle is the inclination of two lines touching one another in a plane, when they do not lie in a straight continuation; and it is rectilinear when the lines containing the angle are straight. When a straight line standing upon a straight line makes the adjacent angles equal to one another, each is called a **right angle** (*angulus rectus*), and the standing line is called the perpendicular or :term:`cathetus`. An obtuse angle is greater than a right angle; an acute angle is less. :just:`[I. Def. 8–10] <I.def.10>`
* **Solid Body** (*Corpus*): A solid is that which possesses length, breadth, and depth (or height), whose boundaries are surfaces. :just:`[XI. Def. 1] <XI.def.1>`

.. assertion:: [I. C.N. 1–5] <I.cn.1>

   Things which are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another; if equals be added to equals, the wholes are equal; if equals be subtracted from equals, the remainders are equal; things which coincide with one another (*quae mutuo sibi invicem congruunt*) are equal to one another; and the whole is greater than its part.

The Agrarian Metrology of Pisa (*De Mensuris Pisanis*)
------------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`De Pertica Pisana (pp. 3–5) <dist-01-pertica-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on the 6 × 6 Pertica Lattice <dist-01-s2-notes>`

.. picture:: /_static/figures/pisan_pertica_grid.svg
   :alt: Pisan Superficial Pertica (6 x 6 Denarii Grid)
   :caption: The Pisan superficial pertica < A B D C > of 6 x 6 linear pedes = 36 superficial denarii, showing the top superficial pes strip < A B P2 P1 > (1 x 6 pedes) and the unit denarius square < C U1 U3 U2 > (1 x 1 pes).

Now the **Pisan rod** (:term:`pertica`), wherewith lands are measured in our city, consists of **six linear feet** (:term:`pedes <pes>`), and each linear foot contains **eighteen ounces or points** (:term:`unciae <uncia>` *sive puncta* $.XVIII.$); thus one linear *pertica* contains $6 \times 18 = 108$ linear ounces.

When, however, a square field :g:`ABDC <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>` has one linear *pertica* on every side—so that :g:`AB <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B P2 P1 >>` and :g:`CD <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>` each measure $6$ linear *pedes*—surveyors do **not** call $1 \text{ pes} \times 1 \text{ pes}$ a superficial foot, lest the fractional parts depart from the duodecimal custom of the rod. Rather, they divide the square :g:`ABDC <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>` by strip-measure:

* **Superficial Pes** (:term:`pes`): A rectangular strip :g:`ABP2P1 <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B P2 P1 >>` of **one linear pertica in length** ($6$ linear *pedes*) by **one linear pes in breadth**, bounded below by the dividing line :g:`P1P2 <pisan_pertica_grid:/ P1 P2 />`. Six such strips fill the whole superficial *pertica* :g:`ABDC <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>`.
* **Superficial Uncia** (:term:`uncia`): A strip of $1$ linear *pertica* by $1$ linear *uncia*, so $18$ superficial *unciae* make $1$ superficial *pes*, and $6 \times 18 = 108$ superficial *unciae* make $1$ superficial *pertica*.
* **Superficial Denarius** (:term:`denarius`): The unit square :g:`CU1U3U2 <pisan_pertica_grid:< C U1 U3 U2 >>` of **$1$ linear pes by $1$ linear pes** at corner :g:`C <pisan_pertica_grid:C>`, bounded by the grid lines through :g:`U1 <pisan_pertica_grid:U1>` and :g:`U2 <pisan_pertica_grid:U2>`. Thus $6$ *denarii* compose one superficial *pes* strip :g:`ABP2P1 <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B P2 P1 >>`, and $6 \times 6 = 36$ *denarii* compose the entire superficial *pertica* :g:`ABDC <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>`, whose perpendicular median axes :g:`EF <pisan_pertica_grid:/ E F />` and :g:`GH <pisan_pertica_grid:/ G H />` meet at the center :g:`I <pisan_pertica_grid:I>` and :eq:`bisect the square equally <pisan_pertica_grid:/ E F / = pisan_pertica_grid:/ G H />`.

At the estate scale, **five and one-half superficial perticae** ($5\frac{1}{2}$ *perticae*) make one :term:`panorum` (a basket-measure of seed-land), and **twelve panora** make one Pisan :term:`stariorum`, which is therefore forty-six and twenty—that is, **sixty-six superficial perticae** ($12 \times 5\frac{1}{2} = 66$ *perticae*, or $2{,}376$ *denarii*).

Distinctio I — Rectangular Fields & Euclidean Geometric Algebra
---------------------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Distinctio I (pp. 5–18) <dist-01-dist1-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Distinctio I <dist-01-s3-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   Here begins the first distinction, on the multiplication of the breadths of rectangular fields into their lengths, in which multiplications their areas are contained.

If you wish to measure an equilateral and equiangular quadrilateral field `.a.b.c.d.` having $2$ *perticae* on each side, its area is that which is gathered from the multiplication of side `.a.c.` into side `.a.b.` contiguous to it, namely two *perticae* into two; thus its area is $4$ superficial *perticae*. Let lines `.a.b.` and `.c.d.` be divided into two equal parts at points `.e.`, `.f.`, and draw line `.e.f.`; likewise let `.a.c.` and `.b.d.` be divided at `.g.`, `.h.`, and draw `.g.h.`. Thus quadrilateral `.a.b.c.d.` is divided into four equal right-angled squares, each of which has one *pertica* on every side; and so there are $4$ superficial *perticae* in the whole area.

Likewise, if an oblong rectangular field `.a.b.g.d.` has breadth $2$ *perticae* and length $3$ *perticae*, dividing the sides into unit *perticae* produces $2 \times 3 = 6$ superficial *perticae*.

When lengths are given in mixed *perticae*, *pedes*, and *unciae*, cross-multiplication obeys the positional table:

* *Perticae* into *perticae* yield superficial *perticae*;
* *Perticae* into *pedes* yield superficial *pedes* ($\frac{1}{6}$ *pertica*);
* *Pedes* into *pedes* yield *denarii* ($\frac{1}{36}$ *pertica*, or $\frac{1}{6}$ superficial *pes*).

.. lemma::

   If a farmer wishes to lay out a rectangular plot of exactly **one Pisan stariorum** ($66$ superficial *perticae*) along a frontage of $7$ *perticae*, divide $66$ by $7$: the required depth is $9\frac{3}{7}$ *perticae*, which in surveyor's subunits is $9$ *perticae*, $2$ *pedes*, and $10\frac{2}{7}$ *unciae*, since $7 \times \left(9 + \frac{2}{6} + \frac{10\frac{2}{7}}{108}\right) = 66$.

To ground all subsequent mensuration upon demonstrative proof, Leonardo appends the rectangular identities of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`'s Book II (:cite:`euclid:elements`) and the chord theorem of Book III:

.. assertion:: [II. 4–6] <II.4>

   1. If a straight line be cut at random into two parts $a$ and $b$, the square on the whole is equal to the squares on the parts and twice the rectangle contained by the parts: $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab$. :just:`[II. 4] <II.4>`
   2. If a straight line `.ab.` be cut into equal parts at `.g.` and into unequal parts at `.d.`, the rectangle contained by the unequal segments `.ad.` and `.db.`, together with the square on the intermediate segment `.gd.`, equals the square on the half `.gb.`: $ad \cdot db + gd^2 = gb^2$. :just:`[II. 5] <II.5>`
   3. If `.ab.` ($10$) be bisected at `.g.` ($5$) and another line `.bd.` ($3$) be added to it in a straight line, then $ad \cdot db + bg^2 = gd^2$ ($13 \times 3 + 25 = 64 = 8^2$), and the sum of the squares $ad^2 + db^2 = 2(ag^2 + gd^2)$ ($169 + 9 = 2(25 + 64) = 178$). :just:`[II. 6, 10] <II.10>`

.. assertion:: [III. 35] <III.35>

   If in a circle two straight lines `.ag.` and `.bd.` cut one another at `.e.`, the rectangle contained by the segments of the one is equal to the rectangle contained by the segments of the other: $ae \cdot eg = be \cdot ed$.
