.. rubric:: *Here begins the sixth distinction, on the measurement of solids.*

The kinds of bodies indeed are several, of which these are: solids, prisms, pyramids, columns, spheres, and their parts, as well as bodies which are called of many bases, and are described about spheres:
properly indeed a solid is that which has breadth, length, and height, and consists of six surfaces: such as dice, chests, cisterns, and similar things.

A prism also is half of a solid,
consisting of three parallelograms and two triangles, which is obtained when a surface cuts a six-based solid along its diagonals, dividing it into two equal portions, each of which is called a prism or wedge.

.. folio:: boncompagni_1862_vol2_practica_geometriae:0166
   :page: 159

A pyramid indeed is a figure drawn from a base, of whatever form it may be, to a single point. A column, however, is a figure raised orthogonally upon a circular base and terminated in a circle which is equal to its base;
or it is that which a rectangular parallelogram sweeps out around one immovably fixed side brought back to the same point; which is also called a round figure. A pyramid of a column is what a right triangle sweeps out around one
of the sides containing the right angle, immovably fixed and brought back to the same point. If therefore the fixed side is equal to the other, it is a right-angled pyramid; if longer, it is acute-angled;
if shorter, it is called obtuse-angled, whose axis is the fixed side and whose base is a circle.

A sphere indeed is a body round on all sides, which we commonly call a ball; and it is that which a semicircle sweeps out around its immovably fixed diameter,
brought back to the same point, whose center is the center of the semicircle, from which all straight lines going out to the surface of the sphere are equal to one another. A hemisphere indeed is
that which embraces half of a sphere, whose base is the greatest circle that can be drawn with compasses in a sphere, whose diameter is the diameter of the sphere, and whose endpoints are called the poles of the sphere.

A portion of a sphere indeed is
that which is more or less than a hemisphere, whose base is a circle smaller than the circle of the hemisphere. Solids of many bases are of many kinds, among which are the solids of 8 bases, and of 12 bases, and of 20 equal bases,
which Euclid in the thirteenth book teaches how to construct within a sphere. There are also infinite other species of bodies, whose measurements will be had through the things that we shall take care to say concerning the aforementioned bodies; and so that the method
of measuring bodies may be had perfectly, we divide this distinction into three parts. In the first of which we shall measure solids of parallel sides, as well as those which upon their bases, of whatever form they may be,
are raised up on high, and terminate in surfaces similar and equal to their bases. In the second also we shall measure pyramids and their parts. In the third indeed we shall measure spheres and their parts,
as well as solids of many bases falling within spheres. And it is to be noted that when we say some body contains so many cubits, or palms, or digits, or inches, or any other measures, then we understand
those measures to be bodily, or cubic, which consist of three equal dimensions, namely breadth, and length, and height. For a bodily or cubic palm is that which has one palm in breadth,
and length, and height, and all angles right: understand the same of cubits and other measures. And so that the things we wish to say may be demonstrated by certain proofs, certain things which are in the eleventh
and remaining following books of Euclid, having been proved, are most clearly set forth, among which are these. A straight line can by no means be in a plane and on high. And when two lines intersect one another, in one
surface they lie. And every triangle is in one surface. And when two plane surfaces intersect one another, their common section is a straight line. And when upon the intersection of two straight lines
some straight line stands perpendicularly, it will stand orthogonally to the whole surface. And when a straight line stands upon the common intersection of three lines, containing a right angle with each of them, these three lines are in one
surface as straight lines; if they are perpendicular to the same surface, they are parallel. Between two parallel lines, however a straight line is joined, it lies in their surface. Of parallel lines, if one is perpendicular to any surface,
the other is perpendicular to the same. Lines parallel to any single line, even though they are not all in the same surface, are also parallel to one another. Two lines containing an angle, if they are parallel to two lines

.. folio:: boncompagni_1862_vol2_practica_geometriae:0167
   :page: 160

containing another angle, even though not in one surface, the angles nevertheless are equal. From one point of any surface it is impossible to erect two perpendiculars to the same surface on the same side.
If two lines containing a surface are parallel to two lines containing another surface, even though not in one surface, these surfaces are parallel. When a surface cuts two parallel surfaces, their common sections
are parallel. If parallel surfaces cut two lines, it is necessary that they be cut proportionally. Whatever surfaces are drawn out from a perpendicular standing upon any surface will stand orthogonally upon that same surface.
Whenever surfaces standing orthogonally upon any surface intersect one another, their common section is perpendicular to the underlying surface. If a solid angle contains three plane angles, any two of them taken together are greater than the remaining one.
All the plane angles which a solid angle contains are less than four right angles. If equal lines contain three plane angles, of which any two taken together are greater than the remaining one, it is possible for a triangle to be made from their chords.
In every solid of parallel surfaces all opposite surfaces are equal, and if a surface cuts a solid of parallel sides parallel to two of its opposite surfaces, it is necessary that it be cut in the ratio of the bases.
If a surface cuts a solid of parallel sides through the diagonal of two of its opposite surfaces, it is necessary that it be cut through the middle. Solids of parallel sides upon the same base and upon one line of the same altitude are equal:
solids of parallel sides upon the same base, and not upon one line, provided they are of the same altitude, are equal: solids of parallel sides upon equal bases with lines drawn up orthogonally from the bases and at the same altitude
are equal. Solids of parallel sides upon equal bases, with lines erected not orthogonally from the bases, provided they are at the same altitude, are equal. Of all solids of parallel sides of the same altitude, the ratio is as base to base. Solids of parallel sides,
if with lines erected orthogonally from the bases they are also equal, their bases and altitudes will be reciprocally proportional. Any solids of parallel sides whatsoever, if they are equal, have their bases and altitudes reciprocally proportional:
and if their bases and altitudes are reciprocally proportional, they themselves will also be equal. Between similar solids of parallel surfaces, the ratio of solid to solid is the triplicate of the ratio of their corresponding sides to one another.
Solids are called similar which have equal angles, and proportional sides about the equal angles. From two equal plane angles, if two lines are erected on high, each of which on both sides contains with the lines of the angles two angles
equal to the two angles which the other similarly contains on either side with the lines of its angle: when from points marked anywhere on the lines erected on high two perpendiculars descend between the surfaces of the angular lines,
then the straight lines joining the points upon which the perpendiculars fall with the angles themselves will also contain equal angles with the erected lines. Between two solids of parallel sides, one of which is contained by three
proportional lines and the other by equal sides, each equal to the mean of the three proportionals, then both their angles become equal and they themselves are equal. If upon proportional lines similar solids of parallel sides are constructed with the same arrangement,
they themselves also become proportional: and if similar solids of parallel sides are proportional, the lines also upon which they are constructed are conversely proved to be proportional.

If all the sides of two opposite surfaces of a cube are divided through the middle, and between the points of division two surfaces are described intersecting the cube and one another, their common section bisects the diagonal of the cube and is bisected by it.
If two wedges, of which the parallelogram of the one is double the triangular base of the other, are of equal height, it is necessary that they be equal. Of all pyramids of the same altitude, whose bases are triangles, when each is divided into two equal pyramids similar to the wholes and into
two equal wedges, the ratio of base to base will be as that of the wedges of the one to the wedges of the other. Of all pyramids whose bases are triangles, if they are of equal height, the ratio to one another is as base to base.
Every wedge can be divided into three equal pyramids of triangular bases. Of all pyramids whose bases are triangles, if they are equal, their bases and altitudes become reciprocally proportional. And if their bases and altitudes
are reciprocally proportional, it follows conversely that those pyramids are equal. Of all similar pyramids of triangular bases, the ratio to one another is the triplicate of the ratio of their corresponding sides to one another. All columns which have one base, or which stand upon
equal bases, have their ratio as axis to axis. Every round column of equal ends and thickness is triple a round pyramid upon the same base of its own altitude. Of every round column and pyramid
which have one base and the same axis, the ratio is the triplicate of the ratio which is between the diameters of their bases. Every pyramid and round column which have one base and the same axis, if they are of equal height with a pyramid and round column which have one
base and the same axis, the ratio both of the pyramids and of the columns will be as base to base. Every pyramid and round column which have one base and the same axis, if they are equal to a pyramid and round column which likewise have one
base and the same axis, their bases and altitudes will be reciprocally proportional. And if their bases and altitudes are reciprocally proportional, it follows conversely that they themselves are also equal. The ratio of all spheres is the triplicate of the ratio of their diameters.
When a line is divided in mean and extreme ratio, if the greater part is produced straight on by an amount equal to half of the divided line, the square of the produced line is fivefold the square of half of the line. If a line is divided into two parts, and the square of the whole
is fivefold the square of the lesser part, then if the greater part is produced up to double the lesser part, the produced line is divided in mean and extreme ratio, of which the greater part is the greater part of the former line.
When a line is divided in mean and extreme ratio, if half of the greater part is adjoined to the lesser part, the square of that line is fivefold the square of half the greater part. When a line is divided in mean and extreme ratio, if it is produced to
an amount equal to its greater part, the whole line also is divided in mean and extreme ratio, of which the greater part is the former line. When a line is divided in mean and extreme ratio, the square of the line together with the square of the lesser part
is triple the square of the greater part. Whenever any rational line is divided in mean and extreme ratio, each part is an apotome. In an equilateral pentagon, if three angles are equal, all the angles will be equal. In a circle,
if an equilateral triangle is inscribed, the square of its side is triple the square of half the diameter. If to the side of a decagon inscribed in a circle the side of a hexagon is joined in a straight line, the whole line is divided in mean and extreme ratio, of which the greater part
is the side of the hexagon. In an equilateral pentagon inscribed in a circle, the pentagonal side is equal in square to the hexagonal side and the decagonal side. In an equilateral pentagon inscribed in a circle, if the chords of two of its angles cut one another, it is necessary that each be cut
at the same point in mean and extreme ratio, and the greater part of each is equal to the pentagonal side.

In every circle whose diameter is rational, the side of the equilateral pentagon is irrational, which is called the minor line. In a sphere whose diameter is rational in length, if a solid of 20 equilateral triangular bases is constructed,
its side is irrational, which is called the minor line. In a sphere whose diameter is rational in square, if a solid of 12 pentagonal bases is constructed, its side is irrational, whose name is the apotome.

.. rubric:: *Here begin the theorems of the fourteenth book.*

The hexagonal side, if it is divided in mean and extreme ratio, has the greater part equal to the decagonal side. A perpendicular drawn from the center of a circle to a pentagonal side is equal to half of the hexagonal side and of the decagonal side.
In a circle the square of the pentagonal side together with the square of the chord of the pentagonal angle is fivefold the square of the hexagonal side. From what has been laid down we undertake to demonstrate that, for a solid of 12 pentagonal bases and one of 20 triangular
bases constructed in the same sphere, the same circle contains the equilateral pentagon and triangle inscribed in the circle; thirty times the surface contained by the pentagonal side and the perpendicular drawn from the center of the circle to the pentagonal side
is equal to the surface of the solid of 12 pentagonal bases. It is of a similar demonstration that, if a circle contains an equilateral triangle, from whose center a perpendicular descends onto the side of the triangle, thirty times the surface contained by the side of the triangle and that perpendicular
is equal to the surface of the solid of 20 triangular bases. From what has been laid down we undertake to show that the ratio of the surface of the solid of 12 pentagonal bases to the surface of the solid of 20 triangular bases constructed in the same sphere is as that of the cubic side
of the same sphere to the side of the solid of 20 triangular bases. Next we undertake to show that the ratio of the cubic side to the side of the solid of 20 triangular bases of the same sphere is as that of a line whose square equals the square of the whole line divided in mean and
extreme ratio together with the square of its greater part, to a line whose square equals the square of the same whole line together with the square of its lesser part: through this it is concluded that the ratio of the cubic side to the side of the solid of 20
triangular bases is the very ratio of the solid of 12 pentagonal bases to the solid of 20 triangular bases. For whatever befalls any line divided in mean and extreme ratio, the same happens to every line similarly divided. After these indeed
proofs we must know the underwritten operations, which are stated in the same books of Euclid, namely: how from a given point on high — from a point marked on high — to draw a perpendicular upon a given surface; from a given
point in a designated surface to erect a perpendicular on high to the same surface. From three plane angles, of which any two taken together are greater than the remaining one, to construct a solid angle: whence it is necessary that those three angles be less than 4
right angles. At a marked point of a given line to construct a solid angle equal to a marked angle contained by lines. Upon a given line to construct a solid similar to a marked solid of parallel surfaces.
Between two circles which are from one center, to describe a many-angled surface by no means touching the lesser circle. Between two spheres which are from one center, to describe a solid of very many surfaces within the greater sphere not touching
the lesser sphere. And within a given sphere to construct solids of 4, and 6, and 8, and 12, and 20 bases; and also to arrange the sides of those five figures together within one sphere:
and to fit certain of the aforementioned five solids within certain others of them: as is taught to be done in the fifteenth book.

When, however, you desire to measure any body of this first part, carefully seek the area of its base, according to what I taught above in the treatise on areas: multiply this by the altitude of the body itself, and what comes forth
will be the volume of that body: for the altitude of bodies of this first part is a straight line erected orthogonally upon the base and terminated in the surface parallel to the base. And so that these things may be clearly shown, let there lie
first a cube .aeg. having 10 palms in breadth, length, and altitude. Whose six surfaces are squares, since they have right angles and equal sides; and let its base be the square .ac., to which is parallel
the square .eg.: and when you multiply the area of the base, which is 100, by line .ac., which is the altitude of the cube, you will have 1000 cubic palms for the volume of the cube itself: and if you wish to have the diagonal of the cube itself, namely line
.hb., join together the squares of sides .ba. and .ad., and you will have 200 for line .db.: to which if the square of line .dh., which is 100, is added, there will be 300 for the square of diagonal .bh.:
therefore diagonal .bh. is the root of three hundred. And it is to be noted that straight line .bh. is the diameter of the sphere in which cube .aeg. falls. And whenever you have knowledge of diagonal .bh., and through it you desire to have the sides of the cube and its volume,
multiply the diameter by itself, and take a third of what comes forth, and you will have the square of the side of the cube; which square, if you multiply it by its root, without doubt the volume of the cube itself will be produced.
And if someone says: I have added the square of the diagonal of a cube with the square of its side, and there came forth 400; I shall posit side .ab. to be a thing, which I shall multiply by itself, and there will come forth a census; which I shall add with its triple,
namely with the square of diagonal .bh., and there will come forth 4 censuses, which are equal to 400 dragmas: divide therefore 400 by 4, and there will come 100 for the square of the side of the cube, which is .ab.; wherefore .ab. is 10.
Again, I have added the square of the diagonal with the side itself, and there came forth 310: I shall again posit side .ab. to be a thing, namely a root; wherefore the square of diagonal .bh. is equal to three censuses, that is, to triple the square of line .ab.: therefore
for the square of the diagonal of the cube and for its side there are had three censuses and one root, which are equal to 310: reduce this to one census, namely take the third part of all the adjacent terms, and there will come forth a census
and a third of a root, which are equal to :math:`103\frac{1}{3}`: wherefore that third of a root must be halved, and there will come forth :math:`\frac{1}{6}`; which multiply by itself, and there will come :math:`\frac{1}{36}`; which add with :math:`103\frac{1}{3}`, and there will be :math:`103\frac{13}{36}`; whose root take,
which is :math:`10\frac{1}{6}`, and subtract from it one sixth for that which you multiplied, and there will remain 10 for side .ab.; whose square, namely 100, triple, and you will have the square of diagonal .bh.: and thus according to this we can find similar
subtleties in cubes. Again let there be a solid whose base is a square having 10 feet on each side; and let its altitude be more or less than the sides of the base; you shall likewise multiply the area of the base by its altitude;

when from its altitude the measure of one foot is taken, and through the boundary of that foot a surface parallel to the base is marked within the solid itself, there will then be as many cubic feet between the base and that surface as there were square feet
in the area of the base. Similarly, if at double one foot of the altitude we mark another surface parallel to the base, there will certainly be between that surface and the base as many cubic feet as there are square feet in double
the base. Similarly, from triple one foot taken upward, a solid is produced in whose volume there will be as many cubic feet in that solid as there were square feet in triple the base:
therefore as many times as the altitude of any solid is a multiple of one foot, so many times are the feet of the volume of the solid itself a multiple of the area of the base. Wherefore when the area of the base is multiplied by the altitude of the solid, the volume of the solid is certainly obtained,
as has been said. And although some solid be not raised by lines erected orthogonally upon the base, nevertheless solids which are upon equal bases and under the same altitude are equal to one another;
the very same thing will be true in it, namely that from the base multiplied into its altitude the volume of the solid will be produced; this same thing can be understood of every body of this first part.

And if we wish to measure some prism, which according to some is called a wedge, we multiply the base, which is triangular,

And it is to be noted that for all bodies of whatever form they may be, when they are similar to one another, the ratio of the volume of the one to the volume of the other will be as the triplicate ratio of a like side of the one to a like side of the other.
For example: if the length of some solid is 2 feet, and the length of another is 3 feet, the ratio of the smaller solid to the larger will be as the triplicate ratio of two to three.
For the triplicate ratio of two to three is as the cube made from two to that made from three, that is as 8 to 27. For as 2 is to 3,
so is 8 to 12, and 12 to 18, and 18 to 27; and thus the ratio from 8 to 27 is the triplicate ratio of two to three. Whence if you know the volume of the smaller body, and multiply it by 27,
and divide the product by 8, without doubt the volume of the larger solid will come forth, etc. And it is to be noted that whatever was said above of solids or of a column, understand this same of cisterns and wells,
since in them are understood to be breadth, length, and depth.

If within a sphere a point be taken from which four straight lines equal to one another meet the surface of the sphere, and their endpoints are not in one plane surface, the point will be the center of the sphere.
For example: let there be a sphere .ab.; and in it let there be point .z., from which four straight lines .zb., .zg., .zd., and .ze. are drawn equal to one another; and let points .b., .g., .d., and .e. not be in one plane surface. I say that point .z. is the center of sphere .ab..

is in one plane, points .b., .g., and .d. will be in one plane, and points .b., .d., and .e. in another; around which points let circles .bgd. and .bde. be drawn with compasses; and from point .z. to the planes of circles .bgd. and .bde.

And since .zi. stands orthogonally upon the plane of circle .b.g.d., the angles .zib., .zig., and .zid. will indeed be right:

When the knowledge of the magnitudes of all the solids falling in one sphere has been obtained through the things that have been said, it will be possible to find the magnitudes of similar solids falling in any other sphere; since the ratio of similar solids is the triplicate ratio of similar sides:
or even of the diameters of the spheres in which those solids have been constructed: for the triplicate ratio of the diameter of one sphere to the diameter of another is that which the cube of the diameter of one sphere has to the cube of the diameter of the other.
Whence if we wish to find the magnitude of the solid of .12. bases falling in a sphere whose diameter is .7., it will be as the cube of six to the cube of seven, that is as .216. to .343., so is the solid found, whose magnitude is :math:`75\frac{1}{5}`, to the magnitude of the sought solid.
