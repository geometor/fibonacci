Prologue & Dedication to Master Michael Scot
--------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Prologus ad Michaelem Scotum (pp. 1–2) <cap-01-prologue-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Michael Scot & Bugia <cap-01-s1-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   Here begins the *Book of Calculation* (*Liber Abaci*), composed by :person:`Leonardo <fibonacci>`, son of Bonaccio, of Pisa, in the year 1202.

You wrote to me, my lord :person:`Master Michael Scot <michael-scot>`, supreme philosopher, that I should transcribe for you the book on number which I formerly composed; yielding therefore to your request and examining it with more subtle scrutiny, I have corrected it to your honor and to the utility of many others. In this correction I added certain necessary matters and pruned away certain superfluous ones. In it I have set forth the full doctrine of numbers according to the manner of the Indians, which method I chose as the most preeminent in that science. And because arithmetic and geometry are sciences connected and mutually supportive of one another, the full doctrine of number cannot be delivered unless certain geometrical matters, or things pertaining to geometry, are interwoven, which here operate solely according to the method of number—a method drawn from many proofs and demonstrations accomplished by geometric figures. Indeed, in another book which I composed on the *Practice of Geometry*, I have explained more copiously those things which pertain to geometry and many others besides, demonstrating each by appended geometric proofs. Truly this book looks more toward theory than toward practice. Wherefore those who wish to know well the practice of this science through it must study diligently in its exercises with continual use and daily practice, so that when knowledge has been turned through practice into habit, memory and intellect may so harmonize with hands and figures that, as though by a single impulse and breath in one and the same instant, all things naturally accord together around the same object; and then, once the student has attained this habit, he will easily be able to advance step by step to the perfection of this art. And so that the doctrine might lie open more readily, I have divided this book into fifteen chapters, so that whatever the reader wishes from these matters he may find more easily. Furthermore, if any insufficiency or defect is found in this work, I submit it to your emendation.

When my father had been appointed by his homeland as public notary in the customs house of :term:`Bugia` for the Pisan merchants assembling there, he had me summoned to him in my boyhood; perceiving future utility and convenience, he wished me to stay there for some days in the study of calculation (*abbaci*) and be instructed. There, having been introduced by marvelous teaching into the art through the nine figures of the Indians, the knowledge of the art pleased me above all others, and I understood it so well that whatever was studied of it in Egypt, Syria, Greece, Sicily, and Provence with their diverse methods—to which places of commerce I afterward traveled—I learned through much study and the contest of disputation. Yet all of this, together with the algorism and the arcs of Pythagoras, I reckoned almost as an error in comparison with the method of the Indians. Wherefore, embracing the method of the Indians more closely and studying it more attentively, adding certain things from my own understanding and also inserting certain subtleties from the geometric art of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, I labored to compose the sum of this book as intelligibly as I could, divided into fifteen chapters, showing nearly everything I inserted by certain proof, so that those seeking this science may be instructed beyond others in the most perfect way, and the Latin people may henceforth never be found lacking it as heretofore. If by chance I have omitted anything more or less than what is right or necessary, I beg pardon, since no one is free from fault or circumspect on every side in all things.

.. folio:: boncompagni_1857_vol1_liber_abbaci:0009
   :page: 2

*Here ends the Prologue. Here begin the Chapters.*

1. On the knowledge of the nine Indian figures, and how every number is written with them; and which numbers, and how, ought to be retained in the hands; and on the introductions to calculation.
2. On the multiplication of whole numbers.
3. On the addition of them to one another.
4. On the subtraction of lesser numbers from greater.
5. On the division of whole numbers by whole numbers.
6. On the multiplication of whole numbers with fractions, and of fractions without wholes.
7. On the addition, subtraction, and division of whole numbers with fractions, and the reduction of parts of numbers into single parts.
8. On the buying and selling of marketable goods and similar things.
9. On barters of marketable goods, the purchase of bullion (*bolsonalia*), and certain similar rules.
10. On partnerships formed among associates.
11. On the alloying of coins and their rules pertaining to alloying.
12. On the solutions of many posed questions which we call miscellaneous (*erraticae*).
13. On the rule of *elcataym* (double false position), how by it nearly all miscellaneous questions are solved.
14. On finding square and cube roots by multiplying, dividing, or extracting them in themselves, and on the treatise of binomials and apotomes (*recisa*) and their roots.
15. On rules pertaining to geometric proportions, and on questions of *algebra* and *almuchabala*.

The Nine Indian Figures and the *Zephirum* (Zero)
-------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`De Cognitione Novem Figurarum (pp. 2–7) <cap-01-numerals-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Positional Place-Value <cap-01-s2-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   *Here begins the first chapter.*

   The nine figures of the Indians are these:

   .. math::

      9 \quad 8 \quad 7 \quad 6 \quad 5 \quad 4 \quad 3 \quad 2 \quad 1

With these nine figures, therefore, and with this sign 0, which in Arabic is called :term:`zephirum`, any number whatsoever is written, as is shown below. For number is an extended collection of units, or a gathering of units, which ascends through its degrees without end. Of these, the first consists of units, which are from one up to ten. The second is made of tens, which are from ten up to one hundred. The third is made of hundreds, which are from one hundred up to one thousand. The fourth is made of thousands, which are from one thousand up to ten thousand, and so of the following degrees without end, each consists of ten times its predecessor. The first degree in writing numbers begins on the right. The second follows the first toward the left. The third follows the second. The fourth follows the third, and the fifth the fourth, and always thus toward the left degree follows degree. Therefore the figure found in the first degree represents itself—that is, if the figure of unity is in the first degree, it represents one; if of two, two; if of three, three, and so in order through those that follow, up to nine. The nine figures that stand in the second degree represent as many tens as they do units in the first—that is, if the figure of unity occupies the second degree, it denotes ten; if of two, twenty; if of three, thirty; if of nine, ninety.

The figure that stands in the third degree denotes as many hundreds as it does tens in the second or units in the first—thus, if the figure of unity, one hundred; if of two, two hundred; if of

.. folio:: boncompagni_1857_vol1_liber_abbaci:0010
   :page: 3

three, three hundred; and of nine, nine hundred. The figure that stands in the fourth degree denotes as many thousands as it does hundreds in the third, or tens in the second, or units in the first; and thus always by changing degree, the number ascends tenfold. And so that what has been said may shine forth more clearly, let it be shown with figures. If the figure of seven is in the first degree and that of three in the second, both together denote 37; or conversely, the figure of three in the first and of seven in the second will denote 73. Again, if the figure of four is in the first and of one in the second, thus 14, they clearly denote XIV; or if the figure of one is in the first and of four in the second, thus 41, they denote XLI. Again, 7 in the first and 2 in the second make 27; for the converse makes 72. If, however, one wishes to write seventy alone, let him place 0 in the first degree and after it place the figure of seven, thus 70; if eighty, let *zephirum* follow the figure of eight, thus 80. By this demonstration, therefore, you can write any number from ten up to one hundred with two figures. With three figures one writes from one hundred up to one thousand: for instance, if the figure of eight is in the first, of five in the second, and of one in the third, 158, they denote one hundred fifty-eight; and conversely, if the figure of one is in the first, of five in the second, and of eight in the third, 851, they denote eight hundred fifty-one; or again, if the figure of eight is in the first, of one in the second, and of five in the third, they denote 518. Again, if by permutation the figure of five is in the first, of eight in the second, and of one in the third, they denote 185. Again, if the figure of one is in the first, of eight in the second, and of five in the third, they clearly denote 581; while three ones, thus 111, make one hundred eleven. But if you wish to write five hundred alone, place *zephira* in the first and second degrees and the figure of five in the third, in this way 500; and thus with two *zephira* you can write any number of hundreds. And if you wish to write hundreds with tens without units, place *zephirum* in the first degree, the tens in the second, and the hundreds you wish in the third. For example, if *zephirum* is put in the first degree, the figure of nine in the second, and of two in the third, they denote 290. If, however, you wish to write hundreds with units without tens, place *zephirum* in the second degree—that is, in the place of the tens—the number of units you wish in the first, and the hundreds in the third: as if the figure of nine is in the first, *zephirum* in the second, and of two in the third, 209; and thus according to the foregoing demonstration you will write whatever number you wish from one hundred up to one thousand with three figures. With four figures one writes from one thousand up to ten thousand, as is shown in the following table with the numbers noted above the figures.

And so one must proceed in the remaining numbers. For with five figures are written all numbers beginning from ten thousand up to one hundred thousand; with six, from one hundred thousand up to one thousand thousand, and so forth—by adding figure to figures, the number ascends step by step tenfold. Wherefore, if it happens that someone is unable to read or understand a number of many figures because of the multitude of figures, I shall take care to show how he ought to read and understand it.

.. folio:: boncompagni_1857_vol1_liber_abbaci:0011
   :page: 4

For the first figure, that is, the figure of the first degree, let him say *one*.

For the second, which is in the second degree, let him say *ten*.

For the third, which will be in the third degree, let him say *one hundred*, and place a mark above it.

For the fourth figure of the same number, let him say *one thousand*, and place a mark beneath it.

For the fifth, let him say *ten thousand*.

For the sixth, *one hundred thousand*, and place a mark above it.

For the seventh, let him say *one thousand thousand*, and again place a mark beneath it.

For the eighth, let him say *ten thousand thousand*.

For the ninth, let him say *one hundred thousand thousand*, and place a mark above it.

For the tenth, let him say *one thousand thousand thousand*, and place a mark beneath it; and so continually by these three orders—namely thousands, ten thousands, and hundred thousands—marking thousands beneath and hundred thousands above, let him take care to mark up to the last degree of the number. And then let him begin to read the number from its last degree by means of the aforesaid marks, saying always for lower marks as many *thousands* as there are lower marks before it toward the first degree, and for upper marks saying *hundred* with as many *thousands* as there are lower marks before it toward the first degree; and for figures that are unmarked after the fourth degree of the number he shall say *ten* with as many *thousands* as there are lower marks before them; and thus he will be able to recognize and read any number of many figures that he wishes.

And so that this may be better understood, let us set forth a certain number of eight figures, 87654321. For 1, which is in the first degree, he will say *one*; for 2, which is in the second, he will say *ten*; for 3, which is in the third, he will say *hundred*, marked above. For 4, which is in the fourth degree, he will say *thousand*, marked beneath, as shown in the prescribed number. For 5, which is in the fifth degree, he will say *ten thousand*; for 6, which is in the sixth degree, he will say *hundred thousand*, marked above; for 7, which is in the seventh degree, he will say *thousand thousand*, marked beneath; for 8, which is in the last degree, he will say *ten thousand*. Therefore in the aforesaid number we have eighty-seven thousand thousand—on account of the two lower marks, one under 7 and the other under 4—and moreover six hundred fifty-four thousand, and moreover 321. Again, let us set forth another number of nine figures, 257604813, which when marked in order is known to contain two hundred fifty-seven thousand thousand, and six hundred four thousand, and eight hundred thirteen. Again, let another number of thirteen figures be set forth, 1007543289081, by whose marks it is known to be one thousand seven thousand thousand thousand, and five hundred forty-three thousand thousand, and two hundred eighty-nine thousand, and moreover eighty-one.

We can also give another easy rule by which you will be able very quickly to read a number of many figures. For example, let us set forth a number of 15 figures, 678935784105296. Leaving aside the first three figures, namely 296, draw a stroke in the form of an arc over each successive group of three as in the preceding example; and for each arc you will say *thousand*, and you will read those three figures which you left aside at the beginning just as they stand. Thus you will say six hundred seventy-eight thousand thousand thousand thousand, since there are four arcs; and nine hundred thirty-five thousand thousand thousand, since there are three arcs above; and seven hundred eighty-four thousand thousand, since

.. folio:: boncompagni_1857_vol1_liber_abbaci:0012
   :page: 5

there are two lines above; and 105 thousand, since there is only one arc; and 296 for those three which you left aside at the beginning. And if at the end one or two figures remain, place them under the last arc, and read all four or all five together; and thus you will be able to read a number of as many figures as you can write.

Once the aforesaid figures and their degrees are well known through frequent use according to the matter described above, it is fitting for those who wish to use the art of calculation—so that they may appear subtler and more ingenious—to know reckoning by the posture of the hands, wisely invented of old according to the custom of the masters of calculation. These signs are as follows. Bending the little finger of the left hand onto the middle of the palm denotes 1. Bending the same finger together with the ring finger similarly onto the middle of the palm denotes 2; with which bending the middle finger denotes 3. Bending the ring and middle fingers onto the middle of the palm denotes 4. Bending the middle finger alone denotes 5; the ring finger alone, 6. Placing the little finger high upon the palm denotes 7; placing the little and ring fingers upon that same spot denotes 8; placing them together with the middle finger upon the same spot denotes 9. Making a circle with the tip of the index finger and the thumb at the joint of the thumb denotes 10. Extending the thumb and index finger so that they touch denotes 20. Making a circle with their tips denotes 30. Placing the thumb over the index finger on the outer side of the index denotes 40. Bending the thumb onto the base of the index finger denotes 50. Bending the index finger over the bent thumb denotes 60. Bending the index finger over the tip of the extended thumb denotes 70. Bending the index finger over the shaft of the extended thumb denotes 80. Again, bending the whole index finger into itself denotes 90. Hundreds and thousands are likewise formed on the right hand in the same order: namely, the sign of unity makes 100 on the right hand, that of two makes 200, that of ten makes 1000, and the sign of ninety makes 9000, as is shown on the following page by pictured hands. All remaining numbers from ten up to ten thousand are therefore composed in the hands with these signs in this way: from the sign of twenty and the sign of three is composed 23; and from the sign of three thousand and the sign of five hundred is composed on the right hand three thousand five hundred; and so understand in the rest.
