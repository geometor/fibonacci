Prologue & Dedication to Master Michael Scot
--------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Prologus ad Michaelem Scotum (pp. 1–2) <cap-01-prologue-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Michael Scot & Bugia <cap-01-s1-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   Here begins the *Book of Calculation* (*Liber Abaci*), composed by :person:`Leonardo <fibonacci>`, son of Bonaccio, of Pisa, in the year $1202$ (revised $1228$).

You wrote to me, my lord :person:`Master Michael Scot <michael-scot>`, supreme philosopher, that I should transcribe for you the book on number which I formerly composed. Yielding therefore to your request, examining it with more subtle scrutiny, I have corrected it to your honor and to the utility of many others. In which correction I added certain necessary matters and resected certain superfluous ones. In it I have set forth the full doctrine of numbers according to the **manner of the Indians** (*iuxta modum Indorum*), which method I chose as the most preeminent in that science.

When my father was appointed by his homeland as public notary in the customs house of Bugia (:term:`Bugia`, Algeria) for the Pisan merchants frequenting it, he had me brought to him in my boyhood; and perceiving the future utility and convenience, he willed that I should abide there for some days in the study of arithmetic (*abbaci*) and be instructed. Where, having been introduced to the art through a wonderful instruction in the **nine figures of the Indians**, the knowledge of the art pleased me beyond all others; so that whatever was studied of it in Egypt, Syria, Greece, Sicily, and Provence with their various methods, I learned through much study and the conflict of disputation.

But all this, as well as the algorism and the arcs of Pythagoras, I counted almost as an error in comparison to the method of the Indians. Wherefore embracing more strictly that method of the Indians, and studying more attentively in it, adding certain things of my own perception and also inserting certain subtleties of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`'s geometric art, I labored to compose this book, distinct in **fifteen chapters**, with almost everything that I inserted shown by certain proof.

The Nine Indian Figures and the *Zephirum* (Zero)
-------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`De Cognitione Novem Figurarum (pp. 2–10) <cap-01-numerals-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Positional Place-Value <cap-01-s2-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   The nine figures of the Indians are these:

   $$9 \quad 8 \quad 7 \quad 6 \quad 5 \quad 4 \quad 3 \quad 2 \quad 1$$

   With these nine figures, and with this sign **$0$**, which is called in Arabic **zephirum** (:term:`zephirum` / *ṣifr*), any number whatsoever is written, as is shown below.

Number is an aggregated collection of units, or a gathering of units, which ascends through its steps into infinity:

1. **The First Degree (Units)**: From one up to ten.
2. **The Second Degree (Tens)**: From ten up to one hundred.
3. **The Third Degree (Hundreds)**: From one hundred up to one thousand.
4. **The Fourth Degree (Thousands)**: From one thousand up to ten thousand; and so on into infinity, each degree being tenfold its antecedent.

The first step in the description of numbers begins from the **right-hand side** (*a dextera*). The second step follows the first toward the **left** (*uersus sinistram*). The third follows the second, the fourth follows the third, and always toward the left step follows step.

Thus, a figure in the first place represents itself: if $1$, one; if $2$, two; if $9$, nine. A figure in the second place represents as many tens as it represents units in the first place: if $1$, ten; if $2$, twenty; if $9$, ninety. A figure in the third place represents as many hundreds: if $1$, one hundred; if $2$, two hundred.

When a place has no value, the sign **$0$** (*zephirum*) is put in that place to hold the rank, so that the preceding and following figures may maintain their proper values and decimal degrees.

.. qed::

   By this positional system, all operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division are performed with speed and absolute mathematical certainty.
