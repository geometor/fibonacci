1. Master Michael Scot and the Court of Frederick II
----------------------------------------------------

The revised $1228$ edition of *Liber Abbaci* opens with a personal dedication to :person:`Master Michael Scot <michael-scot>` (*c. 1175–c. 1232*), court astrologer and philosopher to Holy Roman Emperor :person:`Frederick II <frederick-ii>` Hohenstaufen in Palermo. Scot, who had worked in Toledo translating :person:`Averroës <averroes>` and :person:`Aristotle <aristotle>` from Arabic into Latin, recognized the profound mathematical rigor of Fibonacci's work. The collaboration between Leonardo and the imperial court circle (including Master John of Palermo and Master Dominick) catalyzed the production of *Flos* ($1225$), *Epistola ad Magistrum Theodorum*, and *Liber Quadratorum* ($1225$).

2. The Pisan Customs House at Bugia and Mediterranean Commerce
--------------------------------------------------------------

Leonardo's father, Guglielmo Bonaccio, served as a state official (*scriba publicus in duana*) representing the Republic of Pisa in the port of :term:`Bugia` (modern Béjaïa, Algeria). In the late twelfth century, Bugia was a flourishing intellectual and maritime center of the Almohad Caliphate, renowned for candle wax and trade. It was here that Leonardo was first instructed in the *modus Indorum*. His subsequent commercial and diplomatic travels through Egypt (Alexandria), Syria, the Byzantine Empire (Constantinople), Sicily, and Provence enabled him to compare regional computing methods directly.

3. The "Arcs of Pythagoras" vs. the *Modus Indorum*
---------------------------------------------------

In the prologue, Leonardo notes:

.. epigraph::

   *Sed hoc totum etiam et algorismum atque arcus Pictagorae quasi errorem computavi respectu modi Indorum.*

The "arcs of Pythagoras" (*arcus Pictagorae*) referred to the medieval board abacus (popularized by Gerbert of Aurillac, Pope Sylvester II), where counters marked with apices (ghubār glyphs) were moved across columned boards ruled with arched headings representing powers of ten. In the Gerbertian abacus, empty columns represented zero implicitly by absence of counters; no zero token was written.

Fibonacci recognized that pencil-and-paper calculation on sand or parchment (*algorismus* via the Indian method) superseded physical abacus tables completely because:

1. The :term:`zephirum` ($0$) is treated as a positional symbol equal in operational stature to $1..9$.
2. Carries, borrowings, and intermediate partial products can be inspected, corrected, and archived without losing previous state.
3. Positional notation scales uniformly across fractional operations (:term:`virgula`, common fractions, sexagesimals).

4. Foundation of the *Scuole d'Abaco*
-------------------------------------

While *Liber Abbaci* was composed in Latin for scholars, its practical business algorithms transformed Tuscan vernacular commerce. During the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, Florence, Pisa, and Siena founded *scuole d'abaco* (reckoning schools) to train merchant apprentices in Leonardo's methods. The vernacular *trattati d'abaco* written by Paolo dell'Abbaco, Antonio de' Mazzinghi, and Maestro Benedetto trace their direct pedagogical lineage to the fifteen chapters of *Liber Abbaci*.
