.. rubric:: *Here ends the third distinction; here begins the fourth, on the division of fields among co-heirs.*

We divide the fourth distinction into four parts: in the first of which we shall teach how to divide triangles, in the second quadrilaterals, in the third multilaterals, and in the fourth circles and their segments.

.. rubric:: *Here begins the first part, on the division of triangles.*

When, therefore, you wish to divide any triangle into two equal parts from one of its angles, draw a straight line from that angle to the midpoint of the side subtending it, and you will have what you desire. 
For example: we wish to divide triangle .abg. from point .a. into two equal parts. Let side .bg. be divided into two equal parts at point .d., and let straight line .ad. be joined. I say that triangle .abg. is divided into two equal triangles: 
for triangles .abd. and .adg. are equal to one another, since they are upon equal bases and under the same altitude, which is the drawing of the perpendicular from .a. onto line .bg.: for triangles existing under the same altitude are to one another as their bases, as is held in the beginning of the sixth book. 
Therefore, as .bd. is to .dg., so is triangle .abd. to triangle .adg.: for base .bd. is equal to base .dg.; wherefore triangles .abd. and .adg. are equal to one another, as we said before; 
or if we draw a perpendicular from .a. onto line .bg., that same perpendicular will belong to both triangles .abd. and .adg., equalled by the multiplication of half that same perpendicular by base .bg.

Triangles having one angle equal to one angle have a ratio compounded of the sides containing the equal angles. For the clarity of which, let triangles .abg. and .gez. have equal angles, namely those at .g. 
I say indeed that triangles .abg. and .gez. have a ratio compounded of these ratios which belong to the sides containing the equal angles, namely of that which side .bg. has to side .ge., and of that which side .ag. has to side .gz.; 
which is proved thus: let straight line .ae. be joined, and let triangle .age. be placed between triangles .abg. and .gez.; therefore the ratio of triangle .abg. to triangle .gez. will be compounded of two ratios, namely of that which triangle .abg. has to triangle .age., and of the ratio of triangle .age. to triangle .gez. 
But the ratio of triangle .abg. to triangle .age. is as base .bg. to base .ge., since they are under one altitude; likewise the ratio of triangle .eag. to triangle .egz. is as side .ag. to side .gz.; 
therefore the ratio of triangle .abg. to triangle .gez. is compounded of the ratios of sides .bg. to .ge. and .ag. to .gz., which was to be shown. The ratio of triangle .abg. to triangle .gez. is also compounded of the ratios .bg. to .gz. and .ag. to .ge., 
since sides .ag. and .bg. are antecedents, and sides .ge. and .gz. are consequents: and because, as we said in the treatise on angles, a compounded ratio falls from the product of the antecedents to the product of the consequents, the ratio of triangle .abg. to triangle .gez. will indeed be 
as the product of .bg. by .ag. to the product of .eg. by .gz.: wherefore, if the multiplication of side .eg. by side .gz. is equal to the multiplication of sides .bg. by .ga., triangle .egz. will be equal to triangle .abg.; and if less, less; and if greater, greater; and this I wished to demonstrate.

And if from a triangle a straight line is drawn cutting two sides of the triangle, which with those two sides makes a triangle having one angle in common with the triangle itself, the ratio of one triangle to the other will be as the products of the sides containing that angle. and in it let line .de. be drawn cutting sides .ca. and .cb. at points .ed. I say that triangle .abc. has the ratio to triangle .dec. as the product of .ac. by .cb. to the product of .dc. by .ce.; which is proved thus: upon side .ac. I will apply triangle .acf. equal to triangle .dec.; 
and because triangles .abc. and .afc. are under one altitude, as .bc. is to .fc., so is triangle .abc. to triangle .afc. But the ratio of .ac. to .fc. is as the product of .ac. by .cb. to the product of .ac. by .cf.; therefore the ratio of triangle .abc. to triangle .afc.

.. rubric:: *Here begins the division of quadrilaterals.*

Now the kinds of quadrilaterals are three: namely parallelograms, truncated-head figures, and irregular quadrilaterals. Parallelograms are those whose opposite sides and opposite angles are equal to one another, of which there are four species:
in the first are squares, which have all sides equal and right angles; in the second are rectangles, which have only opposite sides equal to one another and have all right angles; in the third also are rhombi, which consist of four equal sides, yet have unequal angles.
In the fourth are rhomboids, which have only their opposite sides and opposite angles equal to one another; and because there is one way of dividing these four species of parallelograms, I have arranged to label all their figures with the same letters, so that what we say of one of them may be seen to hold for the rest.

for circles are to one another as the squares of their diameters, as is demonstrated in the twelfth book of Euclid: wherefore as the square of line .ea. is to the square of line .ez., so will circle .abcd. be to circle .zit.:
for the square of line .ea. is triple the square of line .ez.; wherefore circle .abcd. is triple circle .zit.; and both circles are parallel, since they have one center.
Therefore its third part has been cut from circle .abcd. by circle .zit. parallel to it; which it was necessary to do.
