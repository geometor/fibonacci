.. rubric:: *Here begins the third distinction, on the measurement of all fields.*

**I have** therefore decided to divide this distinction into five parts. In the first of these we shall measure triangles; in the second, quadrilaterals; in the third, multilaterals, which consist of straight lines; in the fourth, circles and their portions, and moreover oblique figures, and those mixed of straight and curved lines; 
and in the fifth we shall measure fields themselves that lie on the ascent of mountains.

.. rubric:: *Here begins the first part of the third distinction, on the measurement of triangles.*

**Fields** which are triangular or three-sided are called some *orthogonia*, that is right-angled; others *oxigonia*, that is acute-angled; 
others *ampligonii* are also called, and they receive these names from their angles. Indeed an *orthogonium* triangle is one that has one of its three angles right, while the remaining two angles are equal to one right angle. Also an *oxigonium* triangle is one that has all three angles acute. 
An *ampligonium* triangle is one that has one of its three angles greater than a right angle. Triangles also receive names from their sides, of which some are *ysopleuri*, that is equilateral; some *ysocheli*, that is isosceles; and some *diuersilateri*, which are called scalene. 
Equilateral indeed are those whose all three sides are equal to one another. Isosceles are those which have two sides equal to one another. Scalene indeed are those which have all three sides unequal. And it is to be noted that in every triangle three *catheti*, that is perpendiculars, can be drawn; 
of which each falls from any of the angles upon the side subtending or receiving that angle.

Now in right-angled triangles one of the perpendiculars falls within the triangle, and it is that which is produced from the right angle upon the side subtending that angle; 
while the remaining perpendiculars are the two sides containing the right angle. Now in acute-angled triangles all three perpendiculars fall inside. For in obtuse-angled triangles two fall outside and the other inside. *Indeed the area, that is the surface, of all triangles is gathered*

*from the multiplication of half the cathetus into the whole base, or from the multiplication of half the base into the whole perpendicular*; which I shall take care to show with demonstrations. 
If in a triangle from an angle that is not less than any of the remaining angles a cathetus is erected upon the side subtending that angle, it will fall within the triangle. If in triangle .abg. angle .bag. is not less than angle .abg. or .bga., 
I say that if from point .a. upon straight line .bg. a cathetus is erected, it will fall within triangle .abg.

Not so; rather if it is possible, let it fall outside the triangle from side .b.; and let .gb. therefore be produced in a straight line indefinitely onto point .e.; 
and let straight cathetus .az. be drawn upon .ge.; and .azb. will be a right-angled triangle having the angle at .azb. right: and since in triangle .azb. one side has been drawn out outside the triangle, namely .zb. to .bg., indeed the exterior angle at .abg. is greater than the interior opposite angle at .azb.: 
right indeed is the one at .azb.; therefore greater than a right angle is the one at .abg. But the angle at .bag. is not less than that at .abg.; for greater than a right angle is the one at .abg. Likewise also the angle at .bag. is greater than a right angle. 
In triangle .abg. indeed there are two angles greater than two right angles; which is impossible, since all three angles of any triangle are equal to two right angles, as Euclid says in the thirty-second of the first: therefore the cathetus drawn from .a. upon side .bg. does not fall outside the triangle on side .b. 
Similarly it will be shown neither outside on side .g.; for it will fall inside, which was to be shown. From this indeed it is understood that in an acute-angled triangle from the greater angle existing in it, and in a right-angled triangle from the right angle, and in an obtuse-angled triangle from the obtuse angle, 
if a cathetus is erected upon the sides subtending those angles, it will fall within the triangles themselves. Let us therefore demonstrate how in a right-angled triangle the sides containing the right angle are catheti in it. Let there lie indeed a right-angled triangle .bgd., having the angle at .bgd. right. 
I say that straight line .bg. is perpendicular upon straight line .gd., and .dg. upon .bg.: let straight line .gd. be produced in a straight line to point .a.; and since upon straight line .ad. stands straight line .bg., it makes around itself two angles either right or equal to two right angles. 
Right indeed is the angle at .bgd., and right remains the one at .bga. Wherefore .bg. is the cathetus upon .ad. Similarly if straight line .bg. is produced, it will be shown that straight line .dg. is the cathetus upon .bg. I say again: from point .b. another cathetus cannot fall upon straight line .ad. besides cathetus .bg. 
But if it is possible, let .ba. be a cathetus upon .ad., then in triangle .bag. there will be two right angles, which is absurd. Similarly if we make it fall between .g. and .d. upon point .e., there will be in triangle .abe. two right angles, at .bge. and at .geb., which is impossible: 
therefore upon straight line .ad. no cathetus falls besides .bg. Similarly it will be shown that neither from point .d. upon .bg. can a cathetus fall besides .dg.; which was to be shown. If in an acute-angled triangle from a smaller angle existing in it a cathetus is drawn upon the side subtending that angle, 
it will fall within the triangle. For the sake of example: let there be an acute-angled triangle .abg. having a smaller angle at .bag. I say: if from .a. a cathetus is erected upon straight line .bg., it will fall within the triangle. Not so; rather if it is possible, let it fall outside upon point .d. 
And since straight line .ad. is a cathetus upon straight line .dbg., the angle at .adb. is right. But the angle at .abg., which is outside triangle .adb., is greater than the interior opposite angle at .adb. But that at .adb. is right; wherefore the angle at .abg. is greater than a right angle; 
which is absurd, since triangle .abg. is acute-angled: therefore the cathetus from .a. does not fall outside triangle .abg.: for it falls inside; which was to be shown.

In an obtuse-angled triangle, if a cathetus is drawn from an acute angle upon the side subtending that angle, it will fall outside the triangle. 
Let there be an obtuse-angled triangle .bdg., having an obtuse angle at .bdg. I say that if from angle .dbg. a cathetus is drawn upon straight line .gd., it will fall outside triangle .bdg. Not so; rather if it is possible, let it fall between .dg. at point .a. And since .ba. is the cathetus upon straight line .gd., 
the angle at .bad. is indeed right. For the angle at .bda. is greater than a right angle. Wherefore in triangle .bda. there are two angles greater than two right angles; which is impossible. Therefore from point .b. a cathetus upon .gd. does not fall within triangle .bdg. Similarly it will be shown 
that neither from point .g. upon line .bd. can a cathetus fall within triangle .bgd.: for they fall outside; which was to be shown. If upon two lines containing an angle a straight line falls, and in the middle of it a point is taken, and from the point to the angle a straight line is drawn, 
if that straight line is equal to the line lying from the said point up to one of the lines containing the angle, then that angle will be right. For example: let there be two lines .ab. and .bg. containing angle .abg.; and upon them falls straight line .de.; in the middle of which let point .z. be taken, 
and let .zb. be drawn. I say that if straight line .zb. is equal to straight line .zd. or .ze., then angle .abg. is right. Since the three lines .zb., .zd., and .ze. are equal to one another, if from point .z. with the interval of one of them a circle is described, namely passing through points .d.b.e., 
within which circle a certain straight line .de. is fitted, upon which is the center of the circle, therefore straight line .de. is the diameter of that circle, by which diameter the arc .d.b.e. is comprehended: therefore .d.b.e. is a semicircle, within which is angle .dbe. Indeed an angle which is in a semicircle is right, 
as Euclid shows in his third book. From this indeed it is manifest that if straight line .zb. were greater than straight line .zd. or .ze., angle .dbe. would be acute; but if .zb. were less than .zd. or .ze., angle .abg. would be obtuse. 
*Indeed in a right-angled triangle, the square of the side subtending the right angle is equal to the two squares of the sides containing the right angle.* Let there be a right-angled triangle .abg. having a right angle at .agb. I say indeed that the square of line .ab. is equal to the two squares of lines .ag. and .gb.: 
I shall draw upon straight line .ab. from point .g. the cathetus .gd.; and triangle .abg. will be divided into two right-angled triangles, which are .gdb. and .gda.; and they are similar to one another and to the whole, as Euclid demonstrated in the sixth book. 
And since triangle .gdb. is similar to triangle .agb., about the common angle .b. they have proportional sides. For as .db. from triangle .dbg. is to .bg., so .gb. from triangle .bag. is to line .ab. Wherefore the multiplication of .db. into .ba. is equal to the square of line .bg. 
Again, since triangle .gda. is similar to triangle .agb., about their common angle .a. they have proportional sides. Therefore as straight line .da. is to .ag. from triangle .gda., so straight line .ga. from triangle .agb. is to straight line .ab. Wherefore .ad. into .ab. is equal to the square of line .ag. 
Indeed it has been demonstrated that .db. into .ba. is equal to the square of line .gb. Wherefore the multiplication of .db. into .ba. together with the multiplication of line .ad. into .ab. is equal to the two squares of lines .bg. and .ga. 
But the multiplication of .db. into .ab. together with .da. into .ab. is equal to the square of line .ab.; therefore the square of line .ab. is equal to the two squares of lines .bg. and .ga.; which was to be shown. With these things thus demonstrated, how triangles are measured we demonstrate. 
But it is first to be noted that of right-angled and obtuse-angled triangles some

and join line .dc., which will be equal to line .ab. Therefore, because quadrilateral .abcd. is equilateral and right-angled, from which triangle .abc. contains half as is clearly shown in the written figure: therefore the whole square .abcd. is 100 perticas, which are gathered from the multiplication of 10 into 10, 
namely of one side into itself: wherefore triangle .abc., since it is half of that square, must contain 50 perticas. Likewise there is a scalene right-angled triangle .bcd., whose side .bc. is 6 perticas; and side .cd. is 8 perticas; and side .bd. is 10 perticas; and the angle at .c. is right.

And since line .cd. is equidistant and equal to line .df.,

.. rubric:: *Here begins the second difference.*

*The area of all acute-angled triangles is gathered from the multiplication of the cathetus into half the base, or from the multiplication of the base into half the cathetus.* 
For the clarity of which, let there be an acute-angled and equilateral triangle .abc. having in each side 10 perticas: let cathetus .ad. be drawn upon straight line .bc.; since .ad. is a cathetus upon straight line .bc., each of the angles at .d. is equal: therefore triangles .adb. and .adc. are right-angled: 
and since line .ab. is equal to line .ac., and line .ad. is common to both triangles, therefore base .bd. will be equal to base .bc.; and triangle .adb. will be equal to triangle .adc. And since triangle .adb. is right-angled, its area is gathered from the multiplication of cathetus .ad. into half of base .bd., 
namely into 2 1/2. Similarly since triangle .adc. is right-angled, its area is gathered from the multiplication of cathetus .ad. into half of base .dc.; wherefore the area of the whole triangle .abc. is gathered from the multiplication of cathetus .ad. into half of base .bc., as we said above. 
With the same things disposed, it will be shown that the area of triangle .abc. is gathered from the multiplication of the whole base .bc. into half of cathetus .ad. For if you wish to know the length of cathetus .ad., extract the square of line .bd., that is its multiplication, or power, from the power of the whole line .ab., 
namely .25., from .100., there will remain .75.; the root of which number, namely a little less than 8 2/3 perticas, is the length of cathetus .ad.: which 8 2/3 perticas multiplied by half of base .bc., namely by .5., yield a little less than 43 1/3 perticas for the area of the whole triangle .abc. 
Similarly the multiplication of the whole base, namely .10., into half the cathetus, which is a little less than 4 1/3 perticas, makes nearly 43 1/3 perticas; or multiply .bd. by .ad., that is .5. by the root of .75., and you will have the root of .1875. for the area of triangle .abc.; 
whose root is by approximation 43 1/3, less 1/26. And if you wish to find the area of the above-written triangle otherwise from the square of one of the sides, namely of .100., take the third and tenth part; and what results will be the area by approximation. 
For the ratio of the area of any equilateral triangle to the square of its side is a little less than the ratio which .13. has to .30. .. rubric:: *The common way which surveyors ought to use, and it is sufficient in the measurement of all triangles.*

**Let** the surveyor stand over the greater angle of the triangle, and let him look toward the greater side, where from that angle the cathetus ought to fall;
and if he cannot perfectly grasp this by eye, let him have a cord of thread, and fix one end at the angle itself; and let him stretch that thread toward the parts in which the cathetus seems to him to fall; and let him produce it a little beyond the greater side of the triangle;
and then let him move that cord around, until on each side with his hand it touches the greater side itself; and let him mark both points of contact, and divide the space between the two into two equal parts, and there will be the fall of the cathetus:

Quadrilaterals & Multi-Angular Polygons
---------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`De Campis Quadrilateris et Multangulis (pp. 56–87) <dist-03-quad-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Quadrilaterals <dist-03-s3-notes>`

.. rubric:: *Here begins the second part of the third distinction, on the measurement of quadrilaterals.*

**The areas** indeed of quadrilateral fields having right angles are gathered according to what we taught above in another distinction. When these have equal sides, one of the sides is multiplied into itself;
and when they have unequal sides, their lengths are multiplied by their breadths; and thus we have their area. Now the remaining quadrilaterals are divided into four classes; in the first of which are rhombuses, in the second rhomboids, in the third *capita abscissa*,

.. rubric:: *Here begins the second part of the third difference.*

**The remaining** quadrilaterals indeed are divided into four parts: in the first of which are rhombuses, which have all four sides equal to one another, but have angles by no means right.
In the second indeed are rhombuses, that is, those which only have opposite sides equal and equidistant to one another, and also have opposite angles equal.
In the third are fields which have two equidistant sides, but not equal: which are divided into four kinds, according to what we shall demonstrate below.
And in the fourth are *diuersilateri* fields, which have no equidistance in their sides.

When, therefore, you desire to measure any field that lies on the ascent or on the slopes of mountains, diligently inquire into the sides of the surface lying in the plane beneath it, through which take care to have the area of that plane surface.

For that will be the area of the fourth field. For mountains are not measured according to the apparent surfaces in them; since houses, and buildings, trees, and likewise crops do not rise at a right angle upon those surfaces themselves. Hence are sought the areas of those planes upon which the apparent surfaces of mountains lie: and upon which planes all the aforesaid things rise at a right angle. For how through the sides of sloping surfaces you may have knowledge of the sides of the plane surfaces existing beneath them, I wish at present to demonstrate. Let there lie indeed line .ab. for the side of some sloping surface lying on a mountain; and let line .bg. be the side of the plane existing beneath side .ab.; and let straight line .ag. fall perpendicularly upon .bg., making the angle at .g. right: it is indeed necessary through knowledge of the apparent side .ab. to find most truly the hidden side .bg., and likewise knowledge of the perpendicular .ag. Which is done in two ways. For the first way, and that which wise surveyors use, is that at point .a. you place the head of the measuring rod, extending it toward .b. upon line .ab., and hold immovably the head of the rod which is upon .a.: but raise the other upward until the rod itself lies equidistant to line .gb.; which is known through a certain instrument which is called an archipendulum, as I shall demonstrate below: and then through the lower head of the rod let a pebble fall upon line .ab.; and where the pebble falls, there with the same rod begin in the same order to measure with the rod; and thus you will do until you have finished measuring the whole line .ab.
