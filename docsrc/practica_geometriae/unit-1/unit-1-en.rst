Epistle & Prologue to Master Dominicus
--------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Prologus (p. 1) <unit-1-prol-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on the Prologue <unit-1-s1-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   Here begins the *Practice of Geometry* composed by :person:`Leonardo of Pisa <fibonacci>`, of the sons of Bonaccio, in the year $1220$.

You requested, my friend :person:`Dominicus <dominicus>` and reverend Master, that I compose for you a book on the practice of geometry, containing both geometrical demonstrations and the practical usages of measurement according to the custom of surveyors. Moved therefore by the affection of your friendship, and wishing that this work be profitable equally to those who desire to work according to geometrical demonstration and to those who proceed according to lay custom (*secundum vulgarem consuetudinem*), I have labored to set forth this treatise clearly and in order, for which reason I have divided it into **eight distinctions** (*in octo distinctionibus ipsum divisi*):

1. **First Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-1`): On the measurement of equilateral and rectangular quadrangular fields, with the foundational rules of Pisan land measure and the propositions of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`'s second and third books needful to this art.
2. **Second Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-2`): On the finding of square roots (*de inventione radicum*) of integers, fractions, and metrological subdivisions.
3. **Third Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-3`): On the measurement of all planar fields (*in mensuratione omnium camporum*)—triangles, quadrilaterals, multi-angular polygons, and circles.
4. **Fourth Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-4`): On the division of fields among co-heirs or partners (:term:`consortes <consortes>`).
5. **Fifth Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-5`): On the finding of cube roots (*de radicibus cubicis inveniendis*) and their binomial operations.
6. **Sixth Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-6`): On the dimension of corporeal solids (*in dimensione corporum*)—columns, pyramids, cones, spheres, and the five regular solids inscribed in a sphere.
7. **Seventh Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-7`): On finding the heights of elevated things, depths, and planar distances through the quadrant (:term:`quadrans`).
8. **Eighth Distinction** (:cite:`fibonacci:pg-8`): On certain geometrical subtleties (*de quibusdam subtilitatibus geometricis*) pertaining to the pentagon, decagon, and irrational lines.

Introductory Definitions & Common Conceptions
---------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Introductoria (pp. 2–3) <unit-1-intro-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Definitions & Pisan Metrology <unit-1-s2-notes>`

Before entering upon the first distinction, certain principles, petitions, and common conceptions of the mind (*communes animi conceptiones*) from the first book of :person:`Euclid <euclid>` (:cite:`euclid:elements`) must be set down beforehand:

* **Point** (*Punctus*): A point is that which has no part. :just:`[I. Def. 1] <I.def.1>`
* **Line** (*Linea*): Indeed a line is length without breadth, whose extremities are two points; and a straight line is that which lies evenly between its points, namely the shortest extension from one point to another. :just:`[I. Def. 2–4] <I.def.2>`
* **Surface** (*Superficies*): A surface is that which has breadth and length only, whose boundaries are lines; and a plane surface is that which lies evenly between straight lines. :just:`[I. Def. 5–7] <I.def.5>`
* **Rectilinear Angle** (*Angulus planus*): A plane angle is the mutual inclination of two lines touching one another in a plane and not lying in a straight continuation. When a straight line standing upon a straight line makes the adjacent angles equal to one another, each of the equal angles is a **right angle** (*angulus rectus*), and the standing straight line is called the perpendicular or :term:`cathetus` upon that on which it stands. :just:`[I. Def. 8–10] <I.def.10>`
* **Solid Body** (*Corpus*): A solid is that which possesses length, breadth, and depth (or height), whose boundaries are surfaces. :just:`[XI. Def. 1] <XI.def.1>`

.. assertion:: [I. C.N. 1–5] <I.cn.1>

   Things which are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another; if equals be added to equals, the wholes are equal; if equals be subtracted from equals, the remainders are equal; things which coincide with one another (*quae mutuo sibi invicem congruunt*) are equal to one another; and the whole is greater than its part.

The Agrarian Metrology of Pisa (*De Mensuris Pisanis*)
------------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`De Pertica Pisana (pp. 3–4) <unit-1-pertica-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on the 6 × 6 Pertica Lattice <unit-1-s2-notes>`

.. picture:: /_static/figures/pisan_pertica_grid.svg
   :alt: Pisan Superficial Pertica (6 x 6 Denarii Grid)
   :caption: The Pisan superficial pertica < A B D C > of 6 x 6 linear pedes = 36 superficial denarii, showing the top superficial pes strip < A B P2 P1 > (1 x 6 pedes) and the unit denarius square < C U1 U3 U2 > (1 x 1 pes).

Now the **Pisan rod** (:term:`pertica`), wherewith lands are measured in our city, consists of **six linear feet** (:term:`pedes <pes>`), and each linear foot contains **eighteen ounces or points** (:term:`unciae <uncia>` *sive puncta* $.XVIII.$); thus one linear *pertica* contains $6 \times 18 = 108$ linear ounces.

When, however, a square field :g:`ABDC <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>` has one linear *pertica* on every side—so that :g:`AB <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B P2 P1 >>` and :g:`CD <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>` each measure $6$ linear *pedes*—surveyors do **not** call $1 \text{ pes} \times 1 \text{ pes}$ a superficial foot, lest the fractional parts depart from the duodecimal custom of the rod. Rather, they divide the square :g:`ABDC <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>` by strip-measure:

* **Superficial Pes** (:term:`pes`): A rectangular strip :g:`ABP2P1 <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B P2 P1 >>` of **one linearpertica in length** ($6$ linear *pedes*) by **one linear pes in breadth**, bounded below by the dividing line :g:`P1P2 <pisan_pertica_grid:/ P1 P2 />`. Six such strips fill the whole superficial *pertica* :g:`ABDC <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>`.
* **Superficial Uncia** (:term:`uncia`): A strip of $1$ linear *pertica* by $1$ linear *uncia*, so $18$ superficial *unciae* make $1$ superficial *pes*, and $6 \times 18 = 108$ superficial *unciae* make $1$ superficial *pertica*.
* **Superficial Denarius** (:term:`denarius`): The unit square :g:`CU1U3U2 <pisan_pertica_grid:< C U1 U3 U2 >>` of **$1$ linear pes by $1$ linear pes** at corner :g:`C <pisan_pertica_grid:C>`, bounded by the grid lines through :g:`U1 <pisan_pertica_grid:U1>` and :g:`U2 <pisan_pertica_grid:U2>`. Thus $6$ *denarii* compose one superficial *pes* strip :g:`ABP2P1 <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B P2 P1 >>`, and $6 \times 6 = 36$ *denarii* compose the entire superficial *pertica* :g:`ABDC <pisan_pertica_grid:< A B D C >>`, whose perpendicular median axes :g:`EF <pisan_pertica_grid:/ E F />` and :g:`GH <pisan_pertica_grid:/ G H />` meet at the center :g:`I <pisan_pertica_grid:I>` and :eq:`bisect the square equally <pisan_pertica_grid:/ E F / = pisan_pertica_grid:/ G H />`.

At the estate scale, **five and one-half superficial perticae** ($5\frac{1}{2}$ *perticae*) make one :term:`panorum` (a basket-measure of seed-land), and **twelve panora** make one Pisan :term:`stariorum`, which is therefore forty-six and twenty—that is, **sixty-six superficial perticae** ($12 \times 5\frac{1}{2} = 66$ *perticae*, or $2{,}376$ *denarii*).

Distinctio I — Rectangular Fields & Euclidean Geometric Algebra
---------------------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Distinctio I (pp. 5–18) <unit-1-dist1-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Distinctio I <unit-1-s3-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   To find the surface (:term:`embadum`) of any equilateral or oblong right-angled quadrangle, multiply one of the two sides containing a right angle into the other; the product is the sought surface.

If a square `.a.b.c.d.` has $2$ *perticae* on every side, draw through the midpoint of each side parallel lines dividing the square; four unit squares of $1$ *pertica* each are produced ($2 \times 2 = 4$). Likewise, if an oblong rectangle `.a.b.g.d.` has breadth $2$ *perticae* and length $3$ *perticae*, dividing the sides into unit *perticae* produces $2 \times 3 = 6$ superficial *perticae*.

When lengths are given in mixed *perticae*, *pedes*, and *unciae*, cross-multiplication obeys the positional hexagesimal-duodecimal table:

* *Perticae* into *perticae* yield superficial *perticae*;
* *Perticae* into *pedes* yield superficial *pedes* ($\frac{1}{6}$ *pertica*);
* *Pedes* into *pedes* yield *denarii* ($\frac{1}{36}$ *pertica*, or $\frac{1}{6}$ superficial *pes*).

.. lemma::

   If a farmer wishes to lay out a rectangular plot of exactly **one Pisan stariorum** ($66$ superficial *perticae*) along a frontage of $7$ *perticae*, divide $66$ by $7$: the required depth is $9\frac{3}{7}$ *perticae*, which in surveyor's subunits is $9$ *perticae*, $2$ *pedes*, and $10\frac{2}{7}$ *unciae*, since $7 \times \left(9 + \frac{2}{6} + \frac{10\frac{2}{7}}{108}\right) = 66$.

To ground all subsequent mensuration and quadratic equations upon unshakable proof, Leonardo appends the rectangular identities of :person:`Euclid <euclid>`'s Book II (:cite:`euclid:elements`) and the chord theorem of Book III:

.. assertion:: [II. 4–6] <II.4>

   1. If a straight line be cut at random into two parts $a$ and $b$, the square on the whole is equal to the squares on the parts and twice the rectangle contained by the parts: $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab$. :just:`[II. 4] <II.4>`
   2. If a straight line `.ab.` be cut into equal parts at `.g.` and into unequal parts at `.d.`, the rectangle contained by the unequal segments `.ad.` and `.db.`, together with the square on the intermediate segment `.gd.`, equals the square on the half `.gb.`: $ad \cdot db + gd^2 = gb^2$. :just:`[II. 5] <II.5>`
   3. If `.ab.` ($10$) be bisected at `.g.` ($5$) and another line `.bd.` ($3$) be added to it in a straight line, then $ad \cdot db + bg^2 = gd^2$ ($13 \times 3 + 25 = 64 = 8^2$), and the sum of the squares $ad^2 + db^2 = 2(ag^2 + gd^2)$ ($169 + 9 = 2(25 + 64) = 178$). :just:`[II. 6, 10] <II.10>`

.. assertion:: [III. 35] <III.35>

   If in a circle two straight lines `.ag.` and `.bd.` cut one another at `.e.`, the rectangle contained by the segments of the one is equal to the rectangle contained by the segments of the other: $ae \cdot eg = be \cdot ed$.

Distinctio II — On the Finding of Square Roots (*De Inventione Radicum*)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Distinctio II (pp. 18–30) <unit-1-dist2-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Square-Root Extraction <unit-1-s4-notes>`

Because diagonals of rectangles, altitudes of triangles, and sides of regular figures frequently fall upon numbers that are non-square (*numeri non quadrati*), the surveyor must know how to extract square roots both in Hindu-Arabic positional digits (as taught more fully in *Liber Abaci*) and in Pisan subdivisions.

1. **Exact Positional Algorithm**: Drawing upon $(10a + b)^2 = 100a^2 + 20ab + b^2$ :just:`[II. 4] <II.4>`, group the digits in pairs from the right. Subtract the greatest square $a^2$ from the highest pair, double the root found ($20a$) as divisor for the remainder, determine the next digit $b$ such that $(20a + b)b$ does not exceed the remainder, and continue through all pairs.
2. **First & Second Rational Approximations for Surds**: When a remainder $r = N - a^2 > 0$ remains after the integer part $a$, the root lies strictly between $a + \frac{r}{2a + 1}$ and $a + \frac{r}{2a}$. Taking $a_1 = a + \frac{r}{2a}$, its square exceeds $N$ by $\left(\frac{r}{2a}\right)^2$; subtracting $\frac{(r / 2a)^2}{2 a_1}$ yields a second rational convergent accurate to small fractions of an *uncia*:

   $$a_2 = a_1 - \frac{a_1^2 - N}{2 a_1}.$$

3. **Roots of Agrarian Fractions**: To find the side of a square plot whose area is $\frac{2}{3}$ of a superficial *pertica*, reduce $\frac{2}{3}$ *pertica* to unit squares: $\frac{2}{3} \times 36 = 24$ *denarii* (square *pedes*), or $24 \times 324 = 7{,}776$ square *unciae*. Since $88^2 = 7{,}744$ and $88$ linear *unciae* equal $4$ *pedes* and $16$ *unciae* (with remainder $32$, giving $\frac{32}{176} = \frac{2}{11}$ *uncia*), the side of the square plot is $4$ *pedes* and $16\frac{2}{11}$ *unciae*.

.. qed::

   Thus the surveyor is equipped with both geometrical demonstration and arithmetical root extraction before entering upon the measurement of all planar fields.
