Surveying Heights & Distances with the *Quadrans*
-------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Distinctio VII — De Altitudinibus et Quadrante (pp. 202–206) <dist-07-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Optical Similar Triangles <dist-07-s1-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   Here begins the seventh distinction, on finding the heights of elevated things, and the depths and lengths of planes.

If you wish to measure any height: erect a rod upon the plane, and make it stand orthogonally upon that plane; and withdraw yourself from the rod and from the height to be measured; and you shall place your eye upon the ground looking past the top of the rod;
and if your sight passes to the point of the summit of the height to be measured, mark a point upon the ground in the place where your eye will be. And if the line going forth from your eye past the top of the rod does not come to the point of the summit
of that height, move yourself backward or forward until the line proceeding from your eye past the top of the rod ascends straight to the summit of the aforesaid height; and then the ratio of the plane that is between your eye and the elevated object to the elevated object itself that you wish to measure will be
as the plane that is between your eye and the rod is to the rod itself. For example: let the height be *ab*, which stands erect upon the plane in which lies the line *bc*; wherefore angle *abc* will be right; and in that same plane, and orthogonally upon the straight line *bc*, let the rod *de* be erected;
and let point *c* be your eye, from which passes line *ac* ascending through the top of the rod, which is point *e*; and triangle *abc* will be formed from height *ab*, line *bc* lying in the plane, and line *ac* which your eye makes; and triangle *edc* will be formed from rod *ed*, plane *dc*, and line *ce*:

indeed triangles *abc* and *edc* are similar to one another, because they are equiangular; for each of the angles *abc* and *edc* is right, and the angle at *c* is common to both triangles; the remaining angle at *a* is equal to the remaining angle *ced*: therefore triangles *abc* and *dec* are equiangular, and hence similar.
For similar triangles have sides proportional about equal angles; for as *cd* is to *de*, so *cb* is to *ba*. Hence if *cd* and *de*, namely the space between eye and rod and the rod *de* itself, are known, line *cb* will be known, and height *ab* will also be known: because if *cd* equals *de*,
*cb* will equal *ba*; if greater, greater; if lesser, lesser: which things may be shown with numbers. Let rod *ed* be five palms, and space *cd* equal to it, and space *cb* be 30 *ulnae*; on this account height *ab* will likewise be 30 *ulnae*, since *cd* equals *de*, as appears in the first figure.
Again, let *cd* be greater than rod *ed*; on this account plane *cb* will be greater than height *ab*, as appears in this second figure, in which we set space *cd* to 12 palms, rod *ed* to eight palms, and plane *cb* to 60 *ulnae*. Wherefore it will be as *cd* to *de*, that is as 12 to 8,
or in lesser numbers as 3 to 2, so *cb* to *ba*: whence if we multiply 60 by 2 and divide by 3, there come 40 *ulnae* for height *ab*. Again, let *cd* be less than *de*. Wherefore space *cb* will be less than height *ab*, as appears in this third figure, in which we set
*cd* to 9, namely the space between eye and rod, rod *ed* to 12, and plane *cb* to 45. Wherefore by how much *ed* adds above *dc*, by so much will height *ab* add above plane *bc*: for the ratio of *ed* to *dc* is sesquitertian (4 to 3). Wherefore height *ab* adds above plane *cb*
its third part, which is 15; and thus height *ab* is 60: or if 45, namely *cb*, be multiplied by 12, that is by *ed*, and the sum divided by *cd*, namely by 9, there come 60 for height *ab*: or if 1/3 of *cb* be multiplied by 1/3 of *ed* and divided by 1/3 of *cd*, there come likewise 60 for *ab*:
or if 1/3 of *cb* be multiplied by 1/3 of *ed*, there come 60 for height *ab*. From this indeed certain men wishing to measure trees in forests fit for ships adopted such a method: they have a reed equal to their stature, which they have from the tip of the heel up to the eye;
and they place themselves upon the ground facing the tree they wish to measure, lying stretched out while holding the reed orthogonally upright beside the tip of both heels, so that as great as the height of the reed is, so great is the length of their stature from heel to eye;
and they shift themselves sometimes approaching toward the tree, sometimes withdrawing from it; and they do this until their sight passes past the top of the reed to the top of the tree: and then as great as is the length between the eye and the foot of the tree, so great they say is the height of the tree.
For example: let the height of the tree be *ab*, of the reed *cd*, and the stature of the man *de*; and let *e* be his eye, whose sight is line *ae* passing through point *c*; and then *eb* will be as *ed* to *dc*, as was shown above. But geometers, wishing to show some geometric subtlety,
stand not very far from the tree, and with a bow they shoot two arrows at the tree, one to its root and the other to the point of its summit; and to each arrow they tie one thread, and stretch those threads leading them to one point on the plane, making out of those threads and the tree
a right triangle, whose cathetus is the tree itself, and whose base is the thread of the arrow drawn to the foot of the tree, and whose hypotenuse is the thread of the other arrow, which subtends the right angle. For example: let the tree be line *ab*, and the thread of the lower

We can also have the measure of any height by a certain wooden triangle,
provided that in that triangle a cathetus has been drawn from one of the angles, and the base upon which the cathetus falls be placed upon the plane. For example: let the height to be measured be *ab*, and let the wooden triangle be *ecf*, whose cathetus is *ed*; and let triangle *ecf* stand upon the plane of the height,
so that line *ed* stands orthogonally upon that plane; and then let them place the eye upon the side *ec* of the triangle, which eye is *h*, and look through point *e*: and if your sight passing through *e* comes to *a*, it will be as *cd* to *de*, so *cb* to *ba*;
and if your sight passing through *e* comes between *ab*, you will bring the triangle closer to height *ab*; and if that same sight ascends above height *ab*, you will draw the triangle back, and you will always make cathetus *ed* stand orthogonally upon the plane, propping up that triangle with small stones and with earth;
and you will do this until your eye through *e* sees *a*: and when this is done, it will be, as I said, as *cd* to *de*, so *cb* to *ba*: so that if *cd* be 3 of any measure, and *de* be 4, and *cb* 30 paces, on this account height *ab* will be 40 paces;
because *ed* adds above *cd* its third part: wherefore *ab* likewise adds a third above *cb*: or if *cb* be multiplied by *ed*, and the sum divided by *cd*, there come likewise 40 for height *ab*.

And because heights are measured finely, subtly, and easily with the quadrant, which indeed they call the *horoscopus*,
I have taken care for the present to draw the quadrant itself and those things placed upon it that serve our purpose, so that I may more subtly demonstrate what I intend.

I set point *a* as center, and from it I draw two equal straight lines *ab* and *ac* containing a right angle, and with the interval of one of the straight lines *ab* or *ac* I describe the arc *bdc*, extending it slightly outward at point *e*;
moreover I extend line *ab* as far as *g*, and place line *eg* parallel to line *ac*, and divide angle *bac* into two equal parts with line *ad*, and draw from point *d* perpendiculars *dh*, *di* upon straight lines *ab* and *ac*; and from this it will be shown that quadrilateral *dhai* is equilateral and rectangular;
because three of its angles, which are at points *a*, *h*, *i*, are right; the remaining angle at *hdi* is right, since every quadrilateral has four angles equal to four right angles: and because angle *bac* is divided into two equal parts by line *ad*, each of angles *dab* and *dac* will be half a right angle,
for angles *ahd* and *aid* are also right; wherefore each of angles *adh* and *adi* is half a right angle. Wherefore triangles *hda* and *ida* are isosceles, for they are also right-angled: wherefore line *ad*, subtending the right angles of triangles *ahd* and *aid*, is double [in square] of each of the sides *ah*, *dh*, *id*, *ai*;
therefore those four sides are equal to one another: therefore quadrilateral *ahdi* is a square; and at point *a* I fasten a thread with a certain plumb bob, which hangs outside arc *bdc*; and I shall divide each side *dh* and *di* into 12 or 60 equal parts,
and I shall mark all those parts as they are found marked on similar instruments: and thus the form of the quadrant is perfected; and line *eg* is not upon it, for *e* and *g* are pinholes: and if you wish to measure any height with this instrument, place your eye at pinhole *e* and stand facing the height

and the point is observed where the aforesaid thread falls upon lines *hd*, *di*; because if the thread falls upon point *d*, then the height to be measured will be as great as the length between you and the foot of that height, and as much more as your own stature is.
And if the thread falls between points *d* and *i* upon line *di*, say at point *k*, then the ratio of the plane between you and the height to the excess which that height has above your stature will be as *ki* to *ia*, that is as *ki* to *di*; and this is the ratio of a part or parts to its whole:
and if the thread falls upon line *dh*, say at point *l*, then the ratio of the plane to the said remainder of the height to be measured will be as line *ah* to line *hl*, that is as line *hd* to *hl*; and this ratio is of the whole to some part or parts of itself.
And that these things may be demonstrated geometrically, let the thread first fall upon point *d*, which is line *am*, and let *m* be the plumb bob; and because thread *am* hangs from point *a*, which is aloft, that thread will be parallel to the height to be measured, because the thread will stand orthogonally upon the plane
upon which that height stands: and I understand that thread to pass upward until it meets the line which your sight made from *e* passing through *g* to the summit of the height to be measured; and again I understand a line to come from point *e*, namely from your eye, parallel to the plane between the height to be measured,
which will mark on that height a point having in its height from the earth an equality to the height of your stature, and that line with the remainder of the height to be measured and with the line which your eye made passing through *g* to the summit of that same height makes a triangle similar to the triangle
which the thread makes with two sides of the square described in the quadrant. For example: let the height to be measured be *fq*, whose summit is point *q*, to which your sight arrives through *g* from *e*; and let me understand line *en* to be equal to your stature, and likewise *eo* parallel to line *nf*, which is in the plane;
wherefore *of* will be equal to your stature, which is *en*; wherefore triangle *eoq* will be similar to triangle *dia*, which is in the quadrant. To demonstrate which I understand thread *ma* ascending until it meets line *eq* at point *k*; and line *eo* will be parallel to line *ai*. Wherefore the angle *dai* is equal to angle *dke*:
and because thread *adm* is parallel to height *qf*, and straight line *eq* falls upon them, angle *dae* will be equal to angle *fqe*. But angle *dai* was shown equal to angle *dae*; wherefore angle *dai* is equal to angle *oqe*. For angle *eoq* is also equal to angle *aid*:
for each is right, because line *eo* is parallel to line *nf*, and angle *qfn* is right, wherefore angle *eoq* is also right; and angle *aid* is right in the quadrant: therefore the remaining angle *oeq* is equal to the remaining angle *adi*; wherefore triangle *qoe* is similar to triangle *aid*: therefore it is as *di* to *ia*,
so *eo* to *oq*: for *di* is equal to *ia*; wherefore *qo* is also equal to *oe*, that is *fn*; and *fn* is the plane between you and the root of height *fq*: to which if height *fo* be added, that is *en*, namely your stature, height *fq* will be known; and this we wished to demonstrate.
And if the thread falls upon line *di* at point *k*, it will also be shown that triangle *qoe* is similar to triangle *aik*: therefore angle *kai* will be equal to angle *oqe*, and the angles at *o*

that is of *di*, be half, *eo* of *oq* is also half; if a third, a third; if two thirds, two thirds; and so on. And that these may be better understood in numbers: let plane *nf*, that is line *eo*, be 50 *ulnae*; and let *ik* be 37 marks out of the 60 equal marks which are in *di*,
that is in *ai*. Wherefore it will be as 37 to 60, so *eo*, namely 50, to *oq*. Wherefore 50 must be multiplied by 60, and the sum divided by 37, and there come thence :math:`81\frac{3}{37}` *ulnae* for height *oq*; to which if an equal to your stature be added, which is *of*, height *fq* will be known, as was said.


Again, let the thread fall upon side *hd* at point *l*, which is line *alm*; and let thread *ma* be produced to point *r*; and angle *mre* will be equal to angle *oqe*; and angle *mai* will also be equal to angle *oqe*: and because *ahdi* is a square, opposite sides *ai* and *dh* are parallel:
and because straight line *al* falls upon the parallels *ai* and *hd*, angle *lai* will be equal to angle *alh* and to angle *oqe*. Likewise indeed alternately, as is shown in the first book of Euclid, angle *alh* will therefore also be equal to angle *oqe*. For angles *qoe* and *ahl* are also right;
the remaining angle *oeq* remains equal to the remaining angle *hal*. Therefore triangle *qoe* is similar to triangle *ahl*, which is in the quadrant. Now similar triangles have sides proportional about equal angles. Wherefore it is as *ah* to *hl*, so *eo* to *oq*: as many times therefore as *ah* is a multiple of *hl*,
so many times is *eo*, that is plane *nf*, a multiple of *oq*. That these things may be better seen, let *hl* be 39 parts out of the parts of *hd*, which are 60; and let *nf* be 105 cubits: proportionally therefore it will be as 60 to 39; which ratio in lowest numbers is as 20 to 13,
so 105 to height *oq*. Wherefore 13 must be multiplied by the fifth of 105, namely by 21, and the sum divided by the fifth of 20, namely by 4; there will come :math:`68\frac{1}{4}` cubits for height *oQ*.
