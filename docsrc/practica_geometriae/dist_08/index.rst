Distinctio VIII — *Subtilitates Geometricae*: Pentagon, Decagon & *Al-Jabr*
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Distinctio VIII — De Subtilitatibus Geometricis (pp. 207–226) <dist-08-subtilitates-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on Abū Kāmil's Quartic & Euclid XIII.10 <dist-08-s2-notes>`

.. enunciation::

   Here begins the Eighth Distinction, on certain geometrical subtleties (*de quibusdam subtilitatibus geometricis*): to find the chords and sides of the regular **decagon** and **pentagon** inscribed in a circle of rational diameter :math:`d = 10`, both by geometrical proof and by the rule of algebra (*secundum modum algebrae et almuchabalae*).

I wish to find the pentagonal side of a circle of diameter :math:`10`. Let there be a circle *abgde*, and in it an equilateral and equiangular pentagon *abgde*; let the chord *ag* of a pentagonal angle be drawn, and the diameter *bf* bisecting chord *ag* at point *c*. 
Therefore angle *bca* is right; I set diameter *fb* to :math:`10` and pentagonal side *ab* to one *res*, and draw line *af*. Triangle *baf* is right-angled, lying in semicircle *baf*, with perpendicular *ac* drawn from the right angle to the base. Hence *fb* times *cb* equals *ba* squared. 
Let us therefore multiply *ba* (*res*) by itself, giving a *census*; dividing by *fb* (:math:`10`) yields :math:`\frac{1}{10}` *census* for *cb*. Subtracting the square on *cb*, the square of :math:`\frac{1}{10}` *census*, from the *census* leaves one *census* minus :math:`\frac{1}{100}` *census census* for the square on *ac*. 
Since *ag* is double *ac*, the square on *ag* is four times the square on *ac*, namely :math:`4` *census* minus :math:`\frac{1}{25}` *census census*. Since quadrilateral *agde* is inscribed in circle *abgd*, *de* times *ga* plus *gd* times *ea* equals the product of its two diagonals *ge* and *da*. 
Each diagonal equals *ag*, being a pentagonal chord; so *de* times *ag* plus *gd* times *ae* equals *ag* squared, :math:`4` *census* minus :math:`\frac{1}{25}` *census census*. Subtracting the *census* from *gd* times *ae* (*res* times *res*) leaves :math:`3` *census* minus :math:`\frac{1}{25}` *census census* for *de* times *ag*. 
Dividing :math:`3` *census* minus :math:`\frac{1}{25}` *census census* by *res* (*de*) yields :math:`3` *res* minus :math:`\frac{1}{25}` cube for chord *ga*. Squaring :math:`3` *res* minus :math:`\frac{1}{25}` cube gives :math:`9` *census* plus :math:`\frac{1}{625}` cube-of-cube minus :math:`\frac{6}{25}` *census census*, equal to :math:`4` *census* minus :math:`\frac{1}{25}` *census census*. 
Adding :math:`\frac{6}{25}` *census census* to both sides and subtracting :math:`4` *census* leaves :math:`5` *census* plus :math:`\frac{1}{625}` cube-of-cube equal to :math:`\frac{1}{5}` *census census*. Dividing by *census* gives :math:`5` drachmae plus :math:`\frac{1}{625}` *census census* equal to :math:`\frac{1}{5}` *census*. Multiplying by :math:`625` yields one *census census* plus :math:`3125` drachmae equal to :math:`125` *census*. 
Multiply half the *census*, :math:`62\frac{1}{2}`, by itself, giving :math:`3906\frac{1}{4}`; cast out :math:`3125`, leaving :math:`781\frac{1}{4}`. Subtract its root from :math:`62\frac{1}{2}`, leaving :math:`62\frac{1}{2} - \sqrt{781\frac{1}{4}}` for the *census*, whose root is the *res*, namely the pentagonal side.

Otherwise: on diameter *bf*, let us take center *h* and add to radius *bh* its fourth part *hi*, so *bi* is :math:`6\frac{1}{4}`. As proved in Euclid's Thirteenth Book, *bi* is to *ic* as *ic* is to *ih*; hence *bi* is to *ih* as the square on *bi* to that on *ci*. 
For *bi* is five times *ih*, so the square on *bi*, :math:`39\frac{1}{16}`, is five times the square on *ci*; thus *ci* is :math:`\sqrt{7\frac{13}{16}}`. Subtracting this from *bi* leaves :math:`6\frac{1}{4} - \sqrt{7\frac{13}{16}}` for *bc*. Since *fb* is to *ba* as *ba* to *bc*, *ba* squared equals *fb* (:math:`10`) times *bc*, that is, :math:`10` times :math:`6\frac{1}{4} - \sqrt{7\frac{13}{16}}`,

And if you wish to know the side of a decagon in a circle of diameter :math:`10`, divide semicircle arc *abcf* into five equal parts *ab*, *bc*, *cd*, *de*, *ef*; draw chords *ab*, *bc*, *cd*, *de*, *ef*, each a decagon side; and join *ac* and *df*, each a pentagon side. 
Draw lines *ad* and *cf*. Since arc *ac* equals arc *fd*, adding common arc *cd* makes arc *acd* equal to arc *cdf*; hence *ad* equals *cf*. In circle *abg*, quadrilateral *cafd* has diagonals *cf* and *ad*; thus *af* times *cd* plus *ac* times *fd* equals *cf* times *ad*, that is, *cf* squared. 
This understood, set decagon side *cd* as *res*, and multiply it by *af* (:math:`10`), giving :math:`10` *res*; add *ca* times *df*, namely *ca* squared, found above as :math:`62\frac{1}{2} - \sqrt{781\frac{1}{4}}`. We get :math:`10` *res* plus :math:`62\frac{1}{2} - \sqrt{781\frac{1}{4}}` drachmae, equal to *cf* squared. 
Add to this the square on *ca*, :math:`62\frac{1}{2} - \sqrt{781\frac{1}{4}}`, giving :math:`10` *res* plus :math:`125` drachmae minus two roots of :math:`781\frac{1}{4}`, which are :math:`\sqrt{3125}`, equal to :math:`100` drachmae—the square of diameter *af*, since angle *fca* in semicircle *abf* is right. 
Add :math:`\sqrt{3125}` to both sides and subtract :math:`125` from both, leaving :math:`10` *res* equal to :math:`\sqrt{3125} - 25` drachmae. Divide :math:`\sqrt{3125} - 25` by :math:`10`, yielding :math:`\sqrt{31\frac{1}{4}} - 2\frac{1}{2}` drachmae for the *res*, namely the decagon side *cd*.

Otherwise I shall find the decagon and pentagon sides from a known diameter. Let the diameter of circle *abg* be :math:`10`; take center *i*, draw perpendicular *ig*, bisect *if* at *h*, draw *gh* with *hl* equal to *gh*, and join *gl*. I say *il* is the decagon side and *gl* the pentagon side in circle *abfg*. 
This is proved thus: since *fi* is bisected at *h* and *il* is added to it, *il* times *fl* plus *hi* squared equals *hl* squared. Since *gh* equals *hl*, *li* times *fl* plus *hi* squared equals *hg* squared, which equals the squares on *gi* and *ih*. 
Subtracting the square on *ih* in common leaves *il* times *fl* equal to *gi* squared. Since *fi* equals *gi*, *il* times *fl* equals *fi* squared; hence *lf* is to *fi* as *fi* is to *il*. 
Thus line *fl* is divided in extreme and mean ratio, with greater part *fi* the hexagon side and lesser part *il* the decagon side, as in Euclid. Since the pentagon side equals in square the hexagon and decagon sides (*gi* and *il*), *gl* is the pentagon side.

And when you wish to know the side of a pentagon circumscribing a known circle, make known circle *thm* of diameter :math:`10`; let pentagon *abcdf* circumscribe it. From center *e* join *ea*, *eb*, *eh*, and draw chord *th*, the inscribed pentagon side, bisected by *eb* at right angles at *l*. 
The square of chord *th* is :math:`62\frac{1}{2} - \sqrt{781\frac{1}{4}}`; hence the square of its half *tl* is one fourth, :math:`15\frac{5}{8} - \sqrt{48\frac{53}{64}}`. Subtracting this from the square of radius *et* (:math:`25`) leaves :math:`9\frac{3}{8} + \sqrt{48\frac{53}{64}}` for the square on *el*. 
Since triangle *eta* is similar to triangle *etl* (both right-angled, with angles *aet* and *tel* equal), *at* is to *te* as *tl* is to *le*. Since *ab* is double *at* and *th* double *tl*, *ab* is to *te* as *th* to *le*; hence *ab* times *el* equals *te* times *th*. 
Set *ab* as *res*, and multiply its *census* by *el* squared, :math:`9\frac{3}{8} + \sqrt{48\frac{53}{64}}`, giving :math:`9\frac{3}{8}` *census* plus the root of :math:`48\frac{53}{64}` *census census*, equal to *te* squared (:math:`25`) times *th* squared (:math:`62\frac{1}{2} - \sqrt{781\frac{1}{4}}`), which is :math:`1562\frac{1}{2} - \sqrt{488281\frac{1}{4}}`. 
Reduce everything to one *census* by multiplying by :math:`\frac{6}{25} - \sqrt{\frac{4}{125}}` drachmae. Multiplying :math:`\frac{6}{25} - \sqrt{\frac{4}{125}}` into :math:`9\frac{3}{8}` *census* plus the root of :math:`48\frac{53}{64}` *census census* yields one *census*. 
Then multiply :math:`1562\frac{1}{2} - \sqrt{488281\frac{1}{4}}` by :math:`\frac{6}{25} - \sqrt{\frac{4}{125}}` drachmae, giving :math:`375 + \sqrt{15625} - \sqrt{78125} - \sqrt{28125}` drachmae, which I make :math:`500 - \sqrt{200000}`. Thus the *census* equals :math:`500 - \sqrt{200000}`, whose root is *ab*, a side of the pentagon circumscribing the circle of diameter :math:`10`.

Otherwise, more readily: since in right triangle *etb* perpendicular *tl* is drawn from the right angle, *be* squared is to *et* squared as *et* squared to *el* squared; hence *be* squared times *el* squared equals *et* squared times itself, :math:`625`. 
Therefore I set *be* as *res*, square it to *census*, and multiply by *el* squared (:math:`9\frac{3}{8} + \sqrt{48\frac{53}{64}}`), giving :math:`9\frac{3}{8}` *census* plus the root of :math:`48\frac{53}{64}` *census census*, equal to :math:`625` drachmae. Reducing to one *census*—multiplying :math:`625` by :math:`\frac{6}{25} - \sqrt{\frac{4}{125}}`—yields :math:`150 - \sqrt{12500}` for the *census*, *be* squared. 
Subtracting the square on *et* (:math:`25`) leaves :math:`125 - \sqrt{12500}` for the square on *tb*; four times this, :math:`500 - \sqrt{200000}`, is the square of the whole *ab*, as found above.

Having shown how from a known diameter to find the inscribed and circumscribed sides of the pentagon and decagon, it remains to teach how from those sides, when known, to find the diameter—as if we say: the chord of one fifth of a circle is :math:`10`, how long is the diameter?

Likewise, if you wish to find the diameter of a circle whose one-tenth chord is :math:`10`: that chord is to its diameter as any similar chord is to its diameter, and in a circle of diameter :math:`10` the decagon chord is :math:`\sqrt{31\frac{1}{4}} - 2\frac{1}{2}`. 
If you set that unknown diameter as *res* and multiply it by :math:`\sqrt{31\frac{1}{4}} - 2\frac{1}{2}`, you get the root of :math:`31\frac{1}{4}` *census* minus :math:`2\frac{1}{2}` *res*, equal to :math:`100`, the product of the known chord (:math:`10`) and known diameter.

Dividing by :math:`25` gives *census* plus :math:`20` *res* equal to :math:`400` drachmae; by algebra, *res* is :math:`\sqrt{500} + 10`, the sought diameter.

*Flos* (1225) — The Imperial Cubic & Book X Irrationals
-------------------------------------------------------

*Latin Locus*: :ref:`Flos super Solutionibus Quarundam Questionum (pp. 227–234) <dist-08-flos-la>` · *Commentary*: :ref:`Notes on the Flos Cubic & Congruum <dist-08-s3-notes>`

.. rubric:: *Here begins the Flower of Leonardo Bigollo of Pisa upon the solutions of certain questions pertaining to number and to geometry, or to both.*

Having understood, blessed father and venerable lord R., by the grace of God most worthy Cardinal Deacon of Santa Maria in Cosmedin, that you deigned through letters of Your Holiness not merely to command—which better befitted you—but simply to request a copy of my works; nevertheless, reverently receiving that petition as a command, 
I strove most devoutly not only to obey your wish in this part, but also, compiling from a wide multitude the solutions of certain questions posed to me over time by certain philosophers of my most serene lord Caesar and by others, 
and of many solved more subtly than in the greater book on number which I composed, and of many which I myself discovered, I wished this little book composed to the praise and glory of your name to be entitled the *Flower*; 
because I dictated these things to you radiant with the flowery elegance of clerics, and also because therein are set certain flowery though knotty questions, so plausibly unraveled by vigilant investigation both geometrically and arithmetically that they not only flower in themselves, 
nay, even that through them, as little shoots from roots, countless questions emerge, to which if you will sometimes deign to give leisure, you may, if it please you, amidst your cares and occupations, keeping free from that idleness which is the stepmother of virtues, 
under the exercise of the intellect, to seize consolations neither barren, but dutiful. And if I learn that this is accepted by the kindness of your clemency, whatever pleasant subtlety or usefulness I may further discover, I shall dutifully add to this same work, 
so that I may merit to obtain your grace, most affectionately submitting both it and myself to the correction of your lordship.

.. rubric:: *Here ends the prologue; here begins the treatise of the same.*

When before Your Majesty, most glorious Prince Frederick, Master John of Palermo, your philosopher, 
had conferred with me at Pisa about many things concerning numbers, among them he proposed two questions which pertain no less to geometry than to number. The first of these was to find some square number to which, when five is added or subtracted, a square number issues forth; 
which square number, as I reported to the same Master John, I found to be eleven and two thirds and one hundred-forty-fourth of one. The root of this number is three and one fourth and one sixth of one. 
If five be added to this square number, there will come sixteen and two thirds and one hundred-forty-fourth; which number is a square. Its root is four and one twelfth. Likewise if five be taken away from the same square number, there will remain six and two thirds and one hundred-forty-fourth; 
which number is also a square. Its root is two and a third and a fourth of one. And when I had reflected for a long time whence the solution of the aforesaid question arose, I found it to have its origin from many properties that befall square numbers and among square numbers: 
wherefore, taking matter hence, I began to compose a little book to the glory of Your Majesty's highness, which I entitled the *Book of Squares*, wherein will be contained the reasons, proofs, and geometrical solutions of the aforesaid question and many others, which Your Immensity may have if it please Your Highness.

and on this account I studied that tenth book of Euclid more carefully, so much so that I committed its theorems to memory and grasped their meaning. And because it is more difficult than the preceding and certain following books of Euclid, I therefore began to gloss that tenth book itself, 
reducing its meaning to number, which is demonstrated in it through lines and surfaces; which tenth book treats of the varieties of fifteen straight lines, of which fifteen lines two are called rational, or reckoned.

The remaining thirteen are called irrational, or unreckoned. 
Of these two rational ones, one is called rational in length and in power; the other, however, in power alone. By the first of these two is understood a number that can be numbered, as one, two, three, and the rest, or parts of unity, as a half, a third, a fourth, 
and other fractions; all of which are roots of square numbers. By the second are understood the roots of rational non-square numbers. Whence the power of those roots is numbered, while the roots themselves cannot be numbered; and therefore they are called surd numbers. 
And of the thirteen aforesaid lines the first is simple, which is called medial, by which is understood the root of the root of a non-square number. And of the remaining ones, six are roots of binomial numbers, that is, of two names. The rest are roots of apotomes. 
Those of two names are numbers composed of a number and a root, or of two roots; which composition is made in six ways. An apotome number is called the remainder which is between a number and a root, or between two roots; which is also made in six other ways, as Euclid says.

which number exceeds the root itself in power by a line incommensurable with itself in length, that is, that the difference which is between the square of the number itself and the square of the root is not a square number, as happens with four and the root of six.
By the fifth is understood that composed of the root of some non-square number and some number, wherein the square of the root exceeds the square of that number by a quantity not having to the square of the root the ratio of a square number to a square number, as the root of six and two.
For by the sixth is understood that composed of two different roots, of which the greater exceeds the lesser in power by a quantity of a number not having to the square of the greater root the ratio of a square number to a square number, as is that composed of the root of eight and the root of five:
the roots of these six binomials are had in order thus. The root of the first binomial is one of the aforesaid six binomial lines; for when a binomial number is multiplied by itself, surely from that multiplication the first binomial arises. The root of the second binomial is called the first bimedial,
which is composed of two roots of a root commensurable in power only containing a number: that is, when one of them is multiplied by the other, a rational number comes thence: as if the first be the root of the root of :math:`8`,
and the other the root of the root of two, from their multiplication comes the root of the root of :math:`16`, namely :math:`2`. The root of the third binomial is the line called the second bimedial, which is composed of two roots of a root commensurable in power only
containing a medial, namely the root of a number: that is, when one of them is multiplied by the other, that which comes forth is the root of a non-square number: as if the first be the root of the root of :math:`12`, the second the root of the root of three,
from whose multiplication arises the root of six, namely the root of the root of :math:`36`. The root of the fourth binomial is the line called major, which is composed of two lines incommensurable in power, whose squares joined together make a rational number;
and from the multiplication of one by the other comes an irrational number, namely the root of a number: as if the first be the root of :math:`4` and of the root of :math:`13`, and the other be the root of :math:`4` minus the root of :math:`13`. Indeed the root of the fifth binomial
