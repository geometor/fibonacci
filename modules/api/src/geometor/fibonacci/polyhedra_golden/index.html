src.geometor.fibonacci.polyhedra_golden¶

Exact regular polyhedra in a sphere ($d=6$), extreme & mean ratio ($\phi$), Distinctio VIII, Flos, and Liber Abaci.

Grounded directly in Baldassarre Boncompagni’s critical Latin editions (Scritti di Leonardo Pisano, Vol. I, 1857 & Vol. II, 1862):

  • Distinctio VI — The Five Regular Polyhedra in a Sphere of Diameter $6$ (Vol. II, pp. 190–202, fol. 122v–130v): Exact symbolic metrics and straightedge-and-compass planar constructions for the tetrahedron ($e_4^2 = 24$), octahedron ($e_8^2 = 18$), cube ($e_6^2 = 12$), dodecahedron ($e_{12} = \sqrt{15} - \sqrt{3}$, $e_{12}^2 = 18 - \sqrt{180}$), and icosahedron ($e_{20}^2 = 18 - \sqrt{64\frac{4}{5}}$), together with Hypsicles’ Book XIV volume proportion $V_{12} : V_{20} = e_6 : e_{20} = \sqrt{\frac{5 + \sqrt{5}}{6}}$.

  • Distinctio VII — Optical Surveying with the *Quadrans* (Vol. II, pp. 202–206, fol. 131r–133v): Exact geometric model of Fibonacci’s 12-part umbra recta / umbra versa quadrant dhai.

  • Distinctio VIII — *Subtilitates Geometricae* (Vol. II, pp. 207–224, fol. 134r–148r): Inscribing the regular pentagon and decagon in a circle of diameter $10$ both algebraically via Ptolemy’s theorem ($x^4 + 3125 = 125 x^2$) and geometrically via Euclid XIII.9–10.

  • *Flos* (1225) & *Liber Abaci* (1202/1228) Bridges: John of Palermo’s congruum challenge ($x = \frac{41}{12}$, $x^2 \pm 5 = \square$), the cubic $x^3 + 2x^2 + 10x = 20$ solved to six sexagesimal places ($1;22,7,42,33,4,40$), and the twelve-month rabbit recurrence $F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$ whose ratios $F_{n+1}/F_n$ converge monotonically in alternating bounds to $\phi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}$.

Return exact symbolic metrics for all five Platonic solids in a sphere of diameter (default $6$).

Matches Fibonacci’s exact expressions in Practica Geometriae, Distinctio VI (Boncompagni 1862, pp. 190–202) for $d = 6$:

  • Tetrahedron (pyramis): $e_4^2 = 24$, $V_4 = 8\sqrt{3}$.

  • Cube (cubus): $e_6^2 = 12$, $V_6 = 24\sqrt{3} = 3 V_4$.

  • Octahedron (octaedron): $e_8^2 = 18$, $V_8 = 36$.

  • Dodecahedron (duodecaedron): $e_{12} = \sqrt{15} - \sqrt{3}$, $e_{12}^2 = 18 - 6\sqrt{5}$, $r_{f,12}^2 = 6 - \frac{6}{5}\sqrt{5}$, $r_{\text{in},12}^2 = 3 + \frac{6}{5}\sqrt{5}$.

  • Icosahedron (ycosaedron): $e_{20}^2 = 18 - \frac{18}{5}\sqrt{5}$, $r_{f,20}^2 = 6 - \frac{6}{5}\sqrt{5}$, $r_{\text{in},20}^2 = 3 + \frac{6}{5}\sqrt{5}$.

src.geometor.fibonacci.polyhedra_golden.construct_sphere_polyhedra_section() → SpherePolyhedraSectionResult¶

Construct all five regular polyhedron edges inside sphere diameter $AI = 6$ (Euclid XIII.18; Dist. VI).

On great circle (O, A) with $A = (-3, 0)$, $I = (3, 0)$, $O = (0, 0)$:

  • Trisection point $K = (-1, 0)$ ($AK = 2 = \frac{1}{3} AI$) and perpendicular $KB$ to circle give Cube edge $AB$ ($Q = 12$) and Tetrahedron edge $BI$ ($Q = 24$).

  • Center perpendicular $OC$ to $C = (0, 3)$ gives Octahedron edge $AC$ ($Q = 18$).

  • Golden section $N$ of cube edge $[AB]$ gives Dodecahedron edge $AN$ ($Q = 18 - 6\sqrt{5}$).

  • Tangent $[IP] \perp [AI]$ of length $AI = 6$ joined to center $O$ cuts the circle at $M$, giving Icosahedron edge $MI$ ($Q = 18 - \frac{18}{5}\sqrt{5}$).

src.geometor.fibonacci.polyhedra_golden.construct_dodecahedron_golden_chord() → GoldenChordResult¶

Construct Modus diuidendi lineam media et extrema proportione on chord $BE = 2\sqrt{3}$ (pp. 196–197).

Follows Boncompagni pp. 196–197 step-for-step:

  1. Pentagon diagonal / cube edge $BE = \sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$ from $B=(0,0)$ to $E=(2\sqrt{3}, 0)$.

  2. Midpoint $M = (\sqrt{3}, 0)$ and perpendicular $[EC] \perp [BE]$ with $EC = EM = \sqrt{3}$.

  3. Hypotenuse $[BC]$ has $BC^2 = BE^2 + EC^2 = 12 + 3 = 15$ ($BC = \sqrt{15}$).

  4. Circle $(C, E)$ cuts $[BC]$ at $D$, leaving $BD = \sqrt{15} - \sqrt{3}$; circle $(B, D)$ transfers $BD$ onto $[BE]$ at golden cut $G = (\sqrt{15} - \sqrt{3}, 0)$ and meets circle $(E, G)$ at apex $A$ of the dodecahedron’s pentagonal cap $\triangle ABE$ ($AB = AE = BG = \sqrt{15} - \sqrt{3}$).

src.geometor.fibonacci.polyhedra_golden.construct_pentagon_decagon_in_circle() → PentagonDecagonResult¶

Inscribe the regular pentagon & decagon in a circle of diameter $10$ (Distinctio VIII, pp. 207–208).

Follows Fibonacci’s Euclidean demonstration on p. 208 (Euclid XIII.9–10):

  • Circle center $I = (0, 0)$, horizontal diameter $C=(-5,0)$ to $A=(5,0)$, apex $D=(0,5)$ ($R = 5$).

  • Midpoint $H = (\frac{5}{2}, 0)$ of $[IA]$, segment $[HD]$ of length $\sqrt{31\frac{1}{4}} = \frac{5\sqrt{5}}{2}$.

  • Circle $(H, D)$ cuts $[CA]$ at $K = (\frac{5 - 5\sqrt{5}}{2}, 0)$:

    • $IK = \frac{5\sqrt{5} - 5}{2} = \sqrt{31\frac{1}{4}} - 2\frac{1}{2} = s_{10}$ (regular decagon side).

    • $DK = \sqrt{ID^2 + IK^2} = \sqrt{62\frac{1}{2} - \sqrt{781\frac{1}{4}}} = s_5$ (regular pentagon side).

src.geometor.fibonacci.polyhedra_golden.solve_pentagon_ptolemy_quartic(diameter: int | sympy.Expr = 10) → tuple[sympy.Expr, sympy.Expr]¶

Solve Fibonacci’s Distinctio VIII algebraic biquadratic (census census) for pentagon side & diagonal.

In Distinctio VIII (Boncompagni pp. 207–208), setting diameter $d = 10$ and unknown chord $x$ (res, $x^2 =$ census, $x^4 =$ census census), Ptolemy’s theorem on the cyclic pentagon yields $x^4 + \frac{5}{16} d^4 = \frac{5}{4} d^2 x^2$ (for $d = 10$: $x^4 + 3125 = 125 x^2$). Returns (pentagon_side, pentagon_diagonal) as exact nested radicals.

src.geometor.fibonacci.polyhedra_golden.flos_sexagesimal_cubic() → FlosCubicResult¶

Evaluate Fibonacci’s 1225 Flos sexagesimal root $1; 22, 7, 42, 33, 4, 40$ (Boncompagni Vol. II, p. 234).

In Flos, Leonardo Pisano proves against Euclid Book X that no rational or quadratic-irrational line satisfies $x^3 + 2x^2 + 10x = 20$, and gives its root to six sexagesimal places: $1 + \frac{22}{60} + \frac{7}{60^2} + \frac{42}{60^3} + \frac{33}{60^4} + \frac{4}{60^5} + \frac{40}{60^6} = \frac{1596577777}{1166400000}$.

src.geometor.fibonacci.polyhedra_golden.flos_congruum_squares() → tuple[sympy.Rational, sympy.Rational, sympy.Rational]¶

Return John of Palermo’s Flos / Liber Quadratorum rational square triple for $x^2 \pm 5 = \square$.

On Boncompagni Vol. II p. 227, Fibonacci solves $x^2 - 5 = a^2$ and $x^2 + 5 = b^2$ via the congruum $720 = 5 \times 12^2$ (from $(31^2, 41^2, 49^2)$ in arithmetic progression with step $720$), yielding $(a, x, b) = (\frac{31}{12}, \frac{41}{12}, \frac{49}{12})$.
